# Alan Turing — Father of Computer Science

## 1. THE HOOK

He broke the Nazi Enigma code and saved millions of lives. He invented the Turing machine—the foundation of all computing. He was prosecuted for being gay and died at 41. He says: "Sometimes it is the people no one can imagine anything of who do the things no one can imagine."

How does a shy mathematician from London become the father of artificial intelligence—and change the course of history?

## 2. ORIGIN — THE "Before" (The Relatability Engine)

Alan Mathison Turing was born June 23, 1912, in Maida Vale, London. His father was a civil servant in the Indian Civil Service. His mother was the daughter of a railway engineer. His parents were often absent—sent to India for work. Young Alan was raised by a retired army couple.

At Sherborne School, he was mediocre in classics but brilliant in mathematics. He solved problems his teachers could not. He read Einstein's relativity at age 15. He was teased for being different—working on his bicycle instead of playing sports.

In 1931, he won a scholarship to King's College, Cambridge, to study mathematics. He graduated with first-class honors in 1934. In 1936, he published "On Computable Numbers"—the paper that defined the Turing machine. He was 23.

He went to Princeton for two years, studied under Alonzo Church, and returned with a PhD. He was recruited to Bletchley Park in 1938—Britain's codebreaking center.

## 3. THE FIRE — Failures, Rock-Bottom & Turning Points

### Bletchley Park (1938-1945)

Turing's work at Bletchley Park was secret, intense, and decisive. He built the "Bombe"—a machine that decoded German Enigma communications. The Enigma machine had 159 quintillion possible settings. Turing's bombes processed millions of combinations per day. By 1944, British intelligence was reading 90% of German naval communications.

He saved countless lives. The invasion of Normandy was informed by Enigma intercepts. U-boat Wolf Packs were tracked and destroyed. Churchill said Bletchley Park shortened the war by two to four years.

Turing never received public recognition. The work remained classified for 30 years.

### The Manchester Machine (1948)

After the war, Turing moved to Manchester University. He worked on the Ferranti Mark 1—the world's first commercially available computer. He wrote programming manuals. He predicted that computers would eventually think—and that people would talk to them in natural language.

He also fell in love. He met a young man named Arnold Murray in Manchester. In January 1952, his house was burgled. Turing reported the crime and mentioned his relationship with Arnold. Both were arrested.

### The Criminal Conviction (1952)

Turing was charged with "gross indecency"—the same charge that sent Oscar Wilde to prison. He was offered a choice: prison or chemical castration (hormone therapy). He chose hormones. He was also barred from government work and consulting for GCHQ (the successor to Bletchley Park).

His security clearance was revoked. His passport was confiscated. He was forbidden from entering the United States.

He became depressed. He stopped eating. He avoided his friends.

## 4. THE GRIND — The Invisible Years (The Method)

Turing's method was theoretical rigor combined with practical engineering.

### The Turing Machine (1936)

Before computers existed, Turing defined what computation meant. He imagined an infinite tape with symbols. A machine would read symbols, move left or right, and write new symbols. Any computation that could be performed could be done by such a machine.

This abstract model became the foundation of computer science. Every computer, every smartphone, every ATM—each is a physical implementation of Turing's idea.

### The Turing Test (1950)

Turing asked: "Can machines think?" He proposed a test: a human interrogator types questions into a terminal. If a computer can fool the interrogator into thinking it's human, it has passed the test. He predicted that by 2000, computers would have 30% chance of fooling interrogators after five minutes of questioning.

We are still working on this. The test remains controversial. Some say it is too narrow. Others say it is still relevant. But no one has definitively passed it.

### The Patterns Problem

Turing worked on morphogenesis—how patterns in nature form. He proposed reaction-diffusion theory: chemicals spread and interact, creating spots, stripes, spirals. His 1952 paper predicted how leopard spots and seashell spirals form. This work was rediscovered in the 1990s and is now used in computer graphics and robotics.

## 5. THE TEST — THE BREAKTHROUGH MOMENT

### Death (June 7, 1954)

Alan Turing died on June 7, 1954, in his home in Wilmslow, Cheshire. His body was found by his cleaner. An empty cyanide cup was on his bedside table. The coroner ruled it suicide. He was 41 years old.

He left a note: "For thegood of the country" (sic). Some historians believe he was murdered. Others believe it was an accident—he worked with cyanide daily and had poor safety procedures. The truth remains uncertain.

He was not pardoned until 2009—in 2013 the Royal Pardon was granted. In 2021, the Bank of England put his portrait on the new 50-pound note.

## 7. THE LEGACY — Purpose Beyond Profit + Human Side

Turing's legacy is vast. The Turing Award—the Nobel Prize of computing—bears his name. The Turing machine defined computer science. The Turing test defined artificial intelligence.

He was posthumously pardoned by Queen Elizabeth II in 2013. The British government apologized in 2009. Prime Minister Gordon Brown said: "We're sorry. You deserved better."

The 2014 film "The Imitation Game" dramatized his Bletchley Park work. Benedict Cumberbatch played Turing. The film was nominated for eight Academy Awards. It told a partial story—but it brought Turing to millions of people who had never heard of him.
