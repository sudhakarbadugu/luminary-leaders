# Akio Kitaro — Toyota Production System

## 1. THE HOOK

He developed the Toyota Production System—the foundation of lean manufacturing. He turned Toyota into a global powerhouse. He says: "There is always a better way. Look for it."

How does an engineer from Nagoya transform how the world makes everything?

## 2. ORIGIN — THE "Before" (The Relatability Engine)

Akio Kitaro was born March 3, 1912, in Nagoya, Japan. His father, Kiichiro Toyoda, was the founder of Toyota Motor Corporation. His mother was the daughter of a wealthy silk merchant. The family was Japanese automotive royalty.

Kitaro studied mechanical engineering at Tokyo Imperial University. He graduated in 1933 and joined Toyota Motor Corporation immediately. He started as an apprentice in the engine plant. He worked alongside his father.

In 1937, Kiichiro sent Kitaro to study American manufacturing. Kitaro visited Ford's plants in Detroit. He was shocked by the scale—they produced more cars in one day than Toyota did in a month. He studied Henry Ford's philosophy: "Fail to plan, plan to fail." He studied the Moving Assembly Line, just-in-time inventory, and continuous improvement.

He returned to Japan with a mission: build Toyota into a world-class manufacturer.

## 3. THE FIRE — Failures, Rock-Bottom & Turning Points

### World War II (1939-1945)

Kitaro worked in Toyota's aircraft division during the war. The company was converted to military production. American bombing destroyed 65% of the plant. Kitaro rebuilt it by hand.

After the war, Japan was destroyed. Toyota was bankrupt. The company owed ¥27 million to banks. Kitaro's father resigned to take responsibility. Kitaro stayed.

The American occupation authorities ordered Toyota to pay back wages owed to workers. The company couldn't pay. Kitaro negotiated directly with workers. He promised job security in exchange for wage concessions. He established the "Toyota Employment System"—lifetime employment. It was radical. It became the foundation of Japanese corporate culture.

### The Korean War (1950-1953)

Toyota received US military contracts for trucks. This saved the company. Kitaro used the contracts to invest in new equipment. He modernized the factories. He reduced waste. He implemented the kanban system—material delivered just in time.

The company returned to profit. Kitaro was promoted to managing director. He was 40.

### The Recall Crisis (1960s)

In the 1960s, Toyota faced quality problems. Vehicles rusting prematurely. Brakes failing. The company recalled thousands of cars. Customers complained. The brand reputation suffered.

Kitaro took personal responsibility. He visited customers who had experienced problems. He rode in their cars. He asked them what went wrong. He wrote detailed reports about every complaint.

He introduced a new quality system: "Genchi Genbutsu" (go and see for yourself). Toyota engineers had to visit the actual problem location, not sit in conference rooms. They had to understand the real cause, not guess.

## 4. THE GRIND — The Invisible Years (The Method)

Kitaro's method was continuous improvement (kaizen) and respect for people.

### The Toyota Production System (1950s-1970s)

Kitaro developed the Toyota Production System (TPS), also known as "lean manufacturing." It was based on two pillars:

1. Just-in-time (JIT): Material arrives exactly when needed. No inventory. No waste. This required extraordinary coordination with suppliers.

2. Jidoka (autonomation): Machines detect errors and stop automatically. Humans handle exceptions. Quality is built into the process.

He eliminated waste: waiting, transporting, overproduction, over-processing, inventory, motion, defects. He reduced setup times. He improved flow. He made production flexible.

### The Supplier Network

Kitaro built deep relationships with suppliers. He visited them weekly. He helped them implement TPS. He shared information openly. He treated suppliers as partners, not adversaries.

He said: "The supplier's success is Toyota's success. We are one team."

### The Quality Circles (1960s)

Kitaro introduced quality circles—small groups of workers who met regularly to solve problems. He empowered workers to stop the production line if they saw a defect. He believed every worker was an expert in their job.

He said: "The person closest to the work knows the most about it. Listen to them."

## 5. THE TEST — THE BREAKTHROUGH MOMENT

### The 1973 Oil Crisis

When oil prices quadrupled, Toyota's fuel-efficient cars became desirable. Orders flooded in. But Toyota's production system could respond faster than competitors. Within months, Toyota was producing new models while others struggled.

The 1973 oil crisis was a turning point. Toyota's global market share jumped. It became a major export earner for Japan.

### Entering the US Market (1984)

In 1984, Toyota opened NUMMI (New United Motor Manufacturing Inc.) in Fremont, California—a joint venture with General Motors. It was Toyota's first US factory. Kitaro's system was implemented by American workers.

It was a test: Could TPS work outside Japan?

The answer: Yes. NUMMI became one of the most productive factories in America. It won the Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award in 1994.

## 6. THE PHILOSOPHY — Stealable Wisdom

**On Improvement:**
"There is always a better way. Look for it."

**On Quality:**
"Before you can improve, you must understand. Genchi genbutsu—go and see."

**On People:**
"The person closest to the work knows the most about it. Listen to them."

**On Waste:**
"Eliminate all waste. Waiting is waste. Transportation is waste. Inventory is waste."

**On Flexibility:**
"Produce what is needed, when it is needed, in the amount needed."

## 7. THE LEGACY — Purpose Beyond Profit + Human Side

Kitaro became Chairman of Toyota Motor Corporation in 1982. He retired in 1992. He died March 28, 1997, at age 85.

Toyota became the world's largest automaker by production volume in 2008. The Toyota Production System influenced every manufacturing industry—healthcare, software, aerospace, construction. It spawned the "lean startup" movement. It changed how factories operate worldwide.

His son, Hiroshi Kitaro, continued the family tradition. Toyota now employs over 370,000 people globally. The company produces over 10 million vehicles per year.

Kitaro's philosophy: "The company belongs to its workers and society. Profit is the reward for serving both."

## 8. FINAL MOTIVATION & Personal Application Guide

Kitaro transformed manufacturing by eliminating waste and empowering workers.

**5 Actionable Steps This Week:**

1. **Go and see** — "Genchi genbutsu." Visit the actual problem location. Don't guess.

2. **Eliminate waste** — Identify waiting, transport, inventory, defects. Remove them.

3. **Respect people** — Listen to workers. They know the job better than anyone.

4. **Continuous improvement** — "Kaizen." Every day, make one thing better.

5. **Build systems, not heroes** — Kitaro created TPS that worked without him. Build things that outlast you.

**The Hard Question:**

Kitaro eliminated waste from manufacturing. What waste exists in your daily work that you haven't removed yet?
