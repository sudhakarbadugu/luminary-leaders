# John W. Henry — The Trend Follower Who Bought the Red Sox and Broke the Curse

## 1. THE HOOK

He was a trend following trader—a systematic investor who followed price movements without predicting anything. He founded a fund that made money in the 1980s and 1990s by following trends. In 2002, he bought the Boston Red Sox. The team had not won a World Series since 1918—an 86-year drought known as "the Curse of the Bambino." In 2004, the Red Sox won. Then they won again in 2007, 2013, and 2018. John Henry did not know baseball better than anyone else. But he knew data, and he hired people who knew how to use it.

How does a trend-following trader apply systematic thinking to baseball?

## 2. ORIGIN — The "Before" (The Relatability Engine)

John William Henry II was born September 13, 1949, in Quincy, Illinois. His family moved to California. He was interested in farming and music. He played in rock bands. He did not study finance.

He started trading commodities in the 1970s. He was not a fundamental analyst. He did not predict crop yields or supply and demand. He watched prices. When prices went up, he bought. When prices went down, he sold. It was trend following.

He was running from a conventional career. He could have stayed in farming or music. He chose systematic trading.

## 3. THE FIRE — Failures, Rock-Bottom & Turning Points

### John W. Henry & Company (1981)

In 1981, Henry founded John W. Henry & Company (JWH), a managed futures firm that used trend-following strategies. The strategy was systematic: follow trends, diversify across markets, cut losses, let profits run. The firm did well in the 1980s and 1990s.

### The Boston Red Sox (2002-)

In 2002, Henry led a group that bought the Boston Red Sox. The team had not won a World Series since 1918. The sale price was $660 million—then a record for a baseball team.

Henry applied his trading philosophy to baseball. He hired Theo Epstein as general manager. The organization used sabermetrics—statistical analysis of baseball—to value players. The approach was similar to trend following: do not predict; analyze data and act on it.

In 2004, the Red Sox won the World Series. It was their first championship in 86 years. They won again in 2007, 2013, and 2018.

Henry also owns Liverpool Football Club, one of the most storied football clubs in England.

### Challenges at JWH

In the 2000s, trend following struggled. Markets became mean-reverting. Central banks intervened. JWH's performance declined. In 2012, Henry closed the firm to outside investors. He managed only his own money.

## 4. THE GRIND — The Invisible Years (The Method)

Henry's method is defined by trend following, diversification, and discipline.

### Trend Following

Trend followers do not predict. They react. When prices trend upward, they buy. When prices trend downward, they sell. The strategy requires discipline to follow the system even when it loses money.

### Diversification

JWH traded commodities, currencies, interest rates, and stock indices. Diversification reduces the risk that any one market will cause catastrophic losses.

### Discipline

Trend followers must hold positions even when they are losing. Trends take time to develop. If you exit too early, you miss the move. Henry has said that the key to trend following is surviving the drawdowns.

The invisible years—the years of building JWH— were years of systematic trading. Henry did not make discretionary decisions. The rules made the decisions.

## 5. THE TEST — The Breakthrough Moment

The 2004 Red Sox season was the test. The team was down three games to zero in the American League Championship Series against the New York Yankees. They were about to be eliminated. They won four games in a row. They won the American League. They won the World Series. It was one of the greatest comebacks in baseball history. Henry's systematic approach—valuing players, analyzing data—had worked.

## 6. THE PHILOSOPHY — Stealable Wisdom

**On Trend Following:**
Trend following does not try to predict what will happen. It follows what is happening. If prices are rising, the trend is up. If prices are falling, the trend is down.

**On Baseball:**
Henry applied data analysis to baseball. He did not trust intuition. He trusted numbers. If a player gets on base often, he is valuable. It does not matter if he looks like a baseball player.

**On Discipline:**
Trend followers must survive drawdowns. Markets trend and then mean-revert. If you quit during the mean-reversion, you miss the trend.

## 7. THE LEGACY — Purpose Beyond Profit + Human Side

John Henry is still active. He owns the Red Sox and Liverpool FC.

### Wealth

Henry's net worth is estimated at $2.5-3 billion. His wealth has declined from the peak years of JWH.

### Personal Life

Henry has been married multiple times. He has children. He is known for being private and analytical.

### What does he regret?

Henry has not spoken extensively about regrets. The decline of JWH's performance in the 2000s was disappointing to investors. Henry closed the firm to outside investors.

### What does he want to be remembered for?

In interviews, Henry has said he wants to be remembered for the Red Sox championships and for the use of data in sports.

## 8. FINAL MOTIVATION & Personal Application Guide

John Henry's career demonstrates that systematic thinking applies beyond trading. It applies to baseball, football, and any domain with data.

**5 Actionable Steps This Week:**

1. **Follow trends, do not predict** — Henry's trading did not predict market moves. It followed them. If you are trying to predict, you may be overcomplicating.

2. **Diversify** — Trend followers trade multiple markets. If one market is mean-reverting, another may be trending.

3. **Use data, not intuition** — Henry applied sabermetrics to baseball. He did not trust intuition. He trusted numbers. Data is better than opinion.

4. **Survive drawdowns** — Trend following has periods of underperformance. If you quit during the drawdown, you miss the recovery.

5. **Apply your framework beyond your domain** — Henry applied systematic thinking to baseball. It worked. If you have a framework, ask where else it applies.

**The Hard Question:**

If a trend-following trader could break an 86-year curse by applying data analysis to baseball—what domain are you ignoring because you think it is not your field?

The framework applies everywhere.
