# Tiger Woods
## From Child Prodigy to Global Icon

**Born:** December 30, 1975 — Cypress, California  
**Net Worth:** ~$1.1 billion  
**Key Achievements:** 15 Major Championships, 82 PGA Tour Wins, Masters Comeback 2019

---

## Birth and Early Childhood: A Prodigy Emerges

Eldrick Tont Woods — better known as Tiger — was born on December 30, 1975, in Cypress, California, to Earl Woods, a retired U.S. Army officer and former professional baseball player who had played in the Kansas City Royals system, and Kultida Woods, a native of Thailand whom Earl had met while stationed in Bangkok. Earl was a single-digit handicapper and golf enthusiast who recognized exceptional talent in his son before Tiger could fully comprehend the sport. The elder Woods was determined to develop his son's gift, creating a training regimen that combined technical precision, psychological conditioning, and physical development.

Earl Woods began teaching his son golf at the age of three, employing techniques he developed through years of coaching experience. He famously predicted that his son would surpass Jack Nicklaus's record of 18 major championships, a bold proclamation that seemed impossibly ambitious at the time. He created a practice schedule that would have broken lesser children — hundreds of balls per day, competitions against older players, systematic development of every aspect of the game.

Young Tiger practiced obsessively on the challenging par-3 courses near their home in Cypress. He would hit hundreds of balls daily, developing a swing that defied his age. By eight, he was winning junior tournaments against competitors twice his age. His childhood was unusual — structured, disciplined, and almost entirely focused on golf. While other children played freely, Tiger was learning the mental game alongside the physical one. His father taught him that confidence comes from preparation, that pressure is a privilege, and that champions are made in practice, not born.

## Stanford and Amateur Career: Balancing Books and Birdies

Tiger attended Stanford University in 1994 after graduating from high school a year early. He chose Stanford for its academic prestige and proximity to home, planning to study management while pursuing his golf career. He deferred college for two years to focus on the 1994 amateur season, building experience that would serve him well in the transition to professional play. He quickly established himself as the best collegiate golfer in the country, winning the NCAA Championship in 1996 and being named College Player of the Year.

At Stanford, Tiger's dominance was so complete that courses seemed to bend to his will, and fellow competitors described the experience of playing alongside him as intimidating. His focus was absolute — he arrived at courses early, studied every break and slope, and left nothing to chance. He also excelled academically, maintaining a GPA that would have been impressive even without the demands of elite golf. Stanford's environment reinforced his already strong work ethic, pushing him to develop time management skills that would prove essential in his later professional career. He graduated with a degree in economics, though he never seriously considered a career outside golf.

## Professional Breakthrough: Changing the Game

Woods turned professional in late 1996 with a sponsorship deal from Nike reportedly worth $40 million — an unprecedented sum for a rookie. His first major win came at the 1997 Masters, where he won by 12 strokes, the largest margin in Masters history. He was 21 years old, and the golf world understood it was witnessing something unprecedented. His dominance was not just about physical talent — it was about preparation, fitness, and mental toughness. Tiger transformed the physical culture of golf, introducing training regimens that other players had never considered. He lifted weights, stretched daily, and employed sports psychologists to sharpen his focus.

His second major came at the 1999 PGA Championship, where he overcame a one-stroke deficit in the final round to claim victory. This win launched what became known as the "Tiger Slam" — four consecutive major victories spanning 2000 and 2001. By the end of 2000, Tiger had won three majors and established himself as the dominant force in golf. His 2000 season is widely considered the greatest in golf history: he won three majors and nine tournaments, posting scoring records that still stand. He was named Sports Illustrated's Sportsman of the Year.

## The Scandal and Personal Fall: A Public Collapse

In late 2009, reports emerged that Woods had multiple extramarital affairs. Sponsors fled, his carefully constructed public image shattered, and he entered rehabilitation for sex addiction. The revelations were tabloid gold, but the implications extended far beyond media fascination. Woods had built his brand on the integrity of his character, and that foundation had crumbled. He issued a public apology that was widely criticized as insufficient, and his carefully crafted media persona dissolved.

The scandal cost Woods an estimated $22 million in endorsements and dropped him from Forbes' list of highest-paid athletes. His wife, Elin Nordegren, divorced him in 2010 after five years of marriage and two children. He admitted to using prescription medications and seeking treatment for sex addiction. His public statements were rare and carefully crafted, failing to satisfy critics who saw them as insufficiently contrite. The personal toll was enormous — Woods retreated from public life, missing tournaments and avoiding media appearances. For the first time in his career, he looked human — vulnerable, wounded, uncertain of his future.

## Comeback and 2019 Masters: One for the Ages

Woods won his first major in 11 years at the 2019 Masters, finishing with a one-stroke victory after entering the final day trailing by two strokes. The win, witnessed by his children Sam and Charlie, marked one of sports' greatest comebacks. The tears after the final putt were not theatrical — they were genuine expressions of an emotional journey that had nearly ended his career. The ovation from the crowd at Augusta National was one of the most emotional moments in golf history, as spectators who had watched him dominate for decades witnessed his return to the pinnacle.

The significance of the victory extended beyond golf. Woods had endured multiple surgeries, a DUI arrest in 2017 that revealed he had been taking prescription painkillers, and years of public humiliation. He had relearned his swing multiple times, rebuilt his body from damaged components, and found a way to compete at the highest level despite physical limitations that would have ended most athletes' careers. He proved that the human spirit could overcome even the most spectacular fall from grace.

By 2021, Woods was recovered enough to compete in the Masters, despite a 2020 car accident that nearly cost him his right leg. He finished fourth in the 2022 PGA Championship in what may be his final major appearance as a professional. The effort required to compete was visible — he walked with a pronounced limp and relied on a cart between holes — but he was still Tiger Woods, still capable of commanding attention and respect simply by appearing on a golf course.

## Legacy: Transcending Golf

Tiger Woods transformed golf from a gentleman's sport into a global spectacle. He earned over $1.1 billion in career earnings from endorsements, appearances, and tournament winnings, making him one of the highest-earning athletes in history. His influence extended beyond golf: he desegregated the sport's demographics, inspiring millions of new players, many of them from backgrounds that had never considered golf accessible. Before Woods, golf was largely a sport for wealthy white men; after Woods, it became a sport for everyone.

His 15 major championships rank second all-time behind Jack Nicklaus. His dominance from 1999 to 2008 is unmatched in the modern era. Whether his legacy as the greatest golfer of all time will endure remains debated — but few would argue against his status as the most influential golfer of his generation. The Tiger Woods effect is measurable: television ratings, merchandise sales, and tournament attendance all spiked when he competed, and the sport's global popularity grew with his star. He made golf cool, and in doing so, he secured his place in sports history.
