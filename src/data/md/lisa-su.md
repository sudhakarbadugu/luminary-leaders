# Lisa Su — AMD CEO (Alternate Profile)

## 1. THE HOOK
When Lisa Su took over as CEO of AMD in October 2014, the company had just posted a loss of $1.4 billion and its market share in PCs and servers was at historic lows. Its stock was trading below $3. Intel wasdominant, and the conventional wisdom was that AMD would eventually be relegated to the low-end PC market or cease to exist as a major force. Three years later, AMD's Zen architecture had won critical acclaim and market share, its GPUs were powering the fastest supercomputers, and its stock had risen to over $15. By 2023, AMD's market cap had exceeded $100 billion, and Lisa Su had been named "The Best-Performing CEO" by Harvard Business Review. Her turnaround was not just a business story — it was a lesson in how technical conviction and operational discipline can overcome impossible odds.

## 2. ORIGIN — THE "Before" (The Relatability Engine)
Lisa Su grew up in Taiwan, moving to the United States at age 3 when her father's engineering career took the family to Houston. She was a prodigy in mathematics, finishing first in her state math competition in the 6th grade, and she earned a full scholarship to MIT, where she pursued electrical engineering — a field with very few women at the time. She completed her bachelor's, master's, and PhD all at MIT, specializing in semiconductor device design and manufacturing. Her doctoral dissertation on copper interconnect technology positioned her perfectly for a career in chip design.

After MIT, she worked at Texas Instruments in the semiconductor group, then moved to Sun Microsystems, where she worked on SPARC processor architecture. Her engineering background gave her credibility with the technical teams at AMD, and she was known for being able to discuss chip architecture in detail with designers and engineers. She joined AMD in 2012 as senior vice president and general manager of the company's global business units, and she was promoted to CEO in 2014. Her background as both an engineer and a business leader made her unusually effective at aligning technical decisions with market needs.

## 3. THE FIRE — Failures, Rock-Bottom & Turning Points
The early months of Su's tenure were marked by painful decisions. AMD was spread across too many product lines, trying to compete in servers, PCs, graphics, and gaming consoles simultaneously. Su made the controversial decision to exit the server market to concentrate resources on the products with the highest probability of success. This meant abandoning a market where AMD had historically been strong, and it was a calculated risk: if the focused products failed, AMD would have no fallback position.

The Zen architecture project was her赌注. She had personally championed a complete redesign of AMD's processor architecture, a project that required billions of dollars in investment and years of development. The project was behind schedule and over budget, and some internal critics argued that the design was too ambitious and should be abandoned. Su refused to kill the project, and she personally intervened to ensure that the architecture met its performance targets. When Zen launched in 2017, it exceeded expectations: AMD's Ryzen processors delivered performance competitive with Intel at significantly lower price points, and the company's reputation began to recover.

## 4. THE GRIND — The Invisible Years (The Method)
Su's leadership style was characterized by operational rigor and technical engagement. She instituted weekly operational reviews where she personally tracked inventory, yields, and customer demand. She built relationships with the top executives at AMD's key customers — Microsoft, Sony, and the major cloud providers — and she used these relationships to understand market needs and to position AMD's product roadmap accordingly. Her customer focus was unusual for a CEO who was also an engineer, and it gave AMD an advantage in anticipating shifts in demand.

She also invested in manufacturing partnerships that would prove critical to AMD's recovery. The company's relationship with TSMC allowed it to move to 7-nanometer manufacturing ahead of Intel, giving AMD a decisive advantage in power efficiency and performance. Su had championed the TSMC relationship while others at AMD were skeptical, and her willingness to trust a manufacturing partner rather than building internal capacity was a calculated risk that paid off.

## 5. THE TEST — THE BREAKTHROUGH MOMENT
The COVID-19 pandemic created unprecedented demand for computing power, and AMD was uniquely positioned to benefit. The surge in remote work, gaming, and cloud computing drove orders for AMD's CPUs and GPUs to historic levels. Su navigated the supply chain challenges by prioritizing high-margin products like server chips and by working directly with TSMC to secure additional capacity. By 2021, AMD's revenue had grown to over $16 billion, and the company had gained significant market share in servers and PCs.

The data center market was Su's greatest triumph. AMD's EPYC processors, which had barely existed in 2016, were now being used by Google, Amazon, and Microsoft in their cloud infrastructure. The growth was not accidental: Su had personally invested in the product roadmap that made EPYC competitive with Intel's Xeon processors, and she had built the customer relationships that converted design wins into large-scale deployments. By 2023, AMD's data center revenue exceeded $6 billion, and the segment had become the company's largest and most profitable.

## 6. THE PHILOSOPHY — Stealable Wisdom
**On Technical Conviction:**
"You have to believe in your technology when everyone else doubts it. That conviction has to come from deep understanding, not hope."
**On Focus:**
"We can't be everything to everyone. We have to choose where we can win, and then win there."
**On Execution:**
"Strategy without execution is nothing. The details of how you deliver a product matter more than the product's specifications."

## 7. THE LEGACY — Purpose Beyond Profit + Human Side
Lisa Su's legacy is the restoration of competition to the semiconductor industry. Before her tenure, Intel faced no credible challenge in x86 processors, and its dominance had led to slower innovation and higher prices. AMD's resurgence forced Intel to accelerate its own product development and created a competitive dynamic that benefited consumers and businesses worldwide. Su demonstrated that technology leadership requires not just technical knowledge but the ability to translate that knowledge into market-relevant products.

Su is married to a fellow MIT graduate, and they have no children. She is known for her calm, methodical approach to problem-solving and her ability to stay focused during crises. She has been recognized by Fortune, Forbes, and Harvard Business Review as one of the most effective CEOs in technology. She serves on the boards of Cisco and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology Corporation, and she has been an advocate for increasing diversity in engineering. Her personal journey from Taiwan to MIT to the leadership of one of the world's most important technology companies is a testament to the power of education and perseverance.
