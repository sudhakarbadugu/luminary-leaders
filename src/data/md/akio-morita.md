# Akio Morita — Sony Cofounder

## 1. THE HOOK

He cofounded Sony at age 25. He invented the Walkman. He turned Japan into a global electronics power. He says: "You can be totally rational with a machine. But if you work with people, sometimes you have to compromise."

How does a young Japanese physicist build a company that transformed how the world listens to music?

## 2. ORIGIN — THE "Before" (The Relatability Engine)

Akio Morita was born January 27, 1921, in Nagoya, Japan. His family owned a sake brewery. He was the third of five children. His father, Shizuo Morita, was a pharmacist who took over the family business. Akio studied physics at Osaka Imperial University. He graduated in 1945, just as Japan entered its final months of World War II.

He was recruited by the Naval Technical Laboratory to develop a thermal insulation material for submarine sonar domes. The war ended before his work was finished. He was discharged in 1946.

At a radio parts shop in Tokyo, he met Masaru Ibuka. They shared a vision: building electronic products for the consumer market. In 1946, they founded Tokyo Telecommunications Engineering with $19,000 (equivalent to about $260,000 today). Morita was 25. Ibuka was 30.

The early company built rice cookers, heating pads, and religious broadcasting equipment. They failed. They almost went bankrupt. But Morita believed in the future of electronics.

## 3. THE FIRE — Failures, Rock-Bottom & Turning Points

### The Rejecting Tape Recorder (1949)

Sony built a tape recorder based on German technology. Morita tried selling it to NHK, Japan's public broadcaster. NHK rejected it as too inferior to foreign models. Morita kept the prototype and demonstrated it at a school. A teacher bought it. Word spread. The recorder sold—but slowly.

### The Name Problem (1950s)

The company's original name was Tokyo Telecommunications Engineering. Western distributors could not pronounce it. Morita needed an international brand. He combined Latin "sonus" (sound) with "sonny" (a friendly term for young men). The lawyers said "Sony" could not be trademarked in the US as it was too similar to existing companies. Morita changed lawyers and pushed forward.

### The Transistor Radio (1955)

Morita heard about Bell Labs' transistor invention. He flew to America and obtained the license. Sony built the first commercially viable transistor radio—the TR-63—in 1957. It was small, cheap, revolutionary. American teenagers bought 100,000 units.

But Sony was almost bankrupted by the US distributor who sold to anyone without verification. Morita flew to America, dissolved the partnership, and took direct control. He lost $500,000 but saved the brand.

### The Walkman (1979)

Sony's tape recorder business was successful. But Morita wanted a portable music player. Engineers said it was impossible—too thin, too quiet, too expensive. Morita insisted: "If you can't do it, I'll find someone who can." The TPS-L2 launched in July 1979. It weighed 440 grams. It had two headphone jacks. It sold 30,000 units in the first two months. Then millions.

## 4. THE GRIND — The Invisible Years (The Method)

Morita's method was quality obsession and consumer focus.

### The Sony Spirit

Morita established the "Sony Spirit": do what has never been done before. Be a pioneer. He banned the Japanese tradition of nemawashi—informal consensus-building. He said decisions should be made quickly and executed completely. He rewarded innovation. He tolerated failure.

He lived in the same house for 50 years. He ate lunch at the company cafeteria. He took the train. He visited stores personally to see how customers used Sony products.

### Quality Above Profit

Morita once recalled: "We made a commitment that we would never, ever ship a product that wasn't ready." When a batch of Walkman players had minor cosmetic defects, he ordered them destroyed. He would not sell imperfect products under the Sony name.

### Globalization (1950s-1970s)

Morita moved to America in 1960 to establish Sony Corporation of America. He said: "I want to build a company that Americans will trust as their own." He ate American food. He learned English. He wore Western suits. He spent five years learning American business culture.

He hired American executives. He marketed directly to American consumers. Sony's American Depositary Receipts (ADRs) began trading in 1971. He was one of the first Japanese CEOs to truly globalize his company.

## 5. THE TEST — THE BREAKTHROUGH MOMENT

### Trinitron (1968)

Sony's color TV business was failing. The tube technology was inferior to RCA's. Morita ordered a completely new approach: the Trinitron. It used a single gun with three electron beams and a special screen coating. It was brighter, sharper, more expensive. Engineers said it couldn't be done. Morita said: "Do it anyway."

Trinitron launched in 1968. It won every technical award. RCA tried to copy it but failed. Sony dominated the professional monitor market. The Trinitron became synonymous with quality. Sony earned $1 billion from Trinitron over 20 years.

### The Bet-the-Company Decision (1975)

Sony bet everything on the Betamax video format. JVC developed VHS. The battle lasted a decade. Sony lost—VHS dominated. But Morita had bet on quality, not market share. He was wrong. But his philosophy of quality first remained intact.

## 6. THE PHILOSOPHY — Stealable Wisdom

**On Innovation:**
"Our plan is to lead the public with new products rather than ask them what products they want."

**On Quality:**
"If we are not producing the highest quality products, we should not be producing anything."

**On Consumer Focus:**
"The consumer is not looking for cheap products. They are looking for value."

**On Global Expansion:**
"You cannot succeed internationally unless you understand the culture of each country."

## 7. THE LEGACY — Purpose Beyond Profit + Human Side

Akio Morita built Sony into a $20 billion company by the time of his death in 1999. He held 5,600 patents. He pioneered the consumer electronics revolution. He showed that Japanese companies could compete with American giants—and win.

He was appointed to the Japanese Cabinet as Minister of Foreign Affairs in 1979. He advised the US-Japan Business Council. He received the Order of Culture from the Emperor of Japan.

He wrote "Made in Japan" (1990), an autobiography explaining Sony's philosophy. He argued that Japanese companies should move from "copying" to "creating." He was right.

He suffered a stroke in 1992 and retired. He died December 3, 1999, at age 78. Sony now employs 110,000 people worldwide and annual revenue exceeds $80 billion.

## 8. FINAL MOTIVATION & Personal Application Guide

Morita built Sony from nothing by combining quality obsession with consumer understanding.

**5 Actionable Steps This Week:**

1. **Quality over cost** — Morita destroyed defective Walkmans. Your reputation is built product by product.

2. **Learn the culture** — Morita spent years in America learning business culture. Understand your market deeply.

3. **Lead with products** — "Lead the public with new products." Create what people don't know they want yet.

4. **Persist after failure** — Betamax failed. Sony survived. Failure is data, not destiny.

5. **Build for decades** — Sony was founded in 1946. Think in generations, not quarters.

**The Hard Question:**

Morita said machines are rational but people require compromise. Are you treating your team as rational systems—or understanding their human needs?
