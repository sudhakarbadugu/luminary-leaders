# Jesse Livermore — The Boy Plunger Who Made and Lost Fortunes

## 1. THE HOOK

He started trading at age 14 in bucket shops— establishments where you could bet on stock prices without buying stocks. By 15, he had made $1,000—equivalent to $35,000 today. By his 20s, he was known as "The Boy Plunger." He shorted the market before the 1907 panic and made $3 million. He shorted the market before the 1929 crash and made $100 million. He was one of the greatest traders in history. And he lost it all. Multiple times. In 1934, he filed for bankruptcy. In 1940, he took his own life. Jesse Livermore made and lost more fortunes than almost anyone. His rules are still studied. His life is a cautionary tale.

How does a man make a fortune three times and lose it every time?

## 2. ORIGIN — The "Before" (The Relatability Engine)

Jesse Lauriston Livermore was born July 26, 1877, in South Acton, Massachusetts. His family was poor. His father was a farmer. Jesse was not interested in farming. He was interested in numbers.

At age 14, he ran away from home. He went to Boston and got a job posting stock quotes at Paine Webber. Nearby were bucket shops—places where patrons could bet on stock price movements without actually buying securities. Livermore watched the prices. He noticed patterns. He started betting.

He was running from a life of farming. He wanted to make money. And he had a gift for reading price movements.

## 3. THE FIRE — Failures, Rock-Bottom & Turning Points

### The Bucket Shops

In bucket shops, Livermore developed his skill. He watched prices move. He noticed that certain patterns predicted future moves. He bet on them. He won consistently. The bucket shops banned him—he won too often. He moved to New York.

### The Panic of 1907

In 1907, Livermore saw weakness in the stock market. Banks were failing. Trust was eroding. He shorted stocks aggressively. As the market crashed, he made approximately $3 million. J.P. Morgan reportedly asked him to stop shorting—for the good of the financial system.

Livermore was right. But this was not his last fortune. It was his first.

### World War I

Livermore did not only short markets. Before World War I, he went long—believing that the war would drive American industrial production. He was right again.

### The 1929 Crash

Livermore's most famous trade came in 1929. He saw overvaluation in the stock market. He saw margin debt. He shorted aggressively. When the market crashed, he made approximately $100 million—equivalent to over $1 billion today.

### The Losses

But Livermore's story is not only about winning. He lost entire fortunes multiple times. He filed for bankruptcy in 1934. He struggled with depression. His personal life was chaotic—multiple marriages, drinking, gambling. In 1940, he took his own life in the Hotel Pennsylvania in New York City.

## 4. THE GRIND — The Invisible Years (The Method)

Livermore's method was defined by price patterns, discipline (when he followed it), and risk management (when he followed it).

### Price Patterns

Livermore did not trade on fundamentals. He traded on price action. He watched for accumulation and distribution—signs that smart money was buying or selling. He called these "pivotal points."

### Cutting Losses

Livermore's rule: cut losses quickly. He believed that small losses are part of trading. The key is to keep them small and let profits run. He wrote: "It never was my thinking that made the big money for me. It was always my sitting."

### Never Average Down

Livermore's rule: never add to a losing position. Averaging down—buying more as a stock falls—compounds mistakes. If the stock keeps falling, you are wrong. Cut the loss.

### The Discipline Problem

Livermore knew the rules. He did not always follow them. He violated his own principles. He added to losing positions. He let losses grow. He took risks outside of his trading. The difference between knowing the rules and following them is the difference between success and failure.

## 5. THE TEST — The Breakthrough Moment

The 1929 crash was the test. Livermore's short position made him one of the wealthiest people in America. But within five years, he had lost it. He filed for bankruptcy in 1934. The test was not just winning. It was keeping it.

## 6. THE PHILOSOPHY — Stealable Wisdom

**On Sitting:**
"It never was my thinking that made the big money for me. It was always my sitting." Livermore believed that once you have a position, you must sit. Do not exit too early. Let the trade unfold.

**On Cutting Losses:**
"If a trade goes against you, get out. Do not hope. Do not wait. Get out." Livermore knew that small losses are better than large ones. He did not always follow his own advice.

**On Averaging Down:**
"Never average down." Adding to a losing position is a mistake. If the trade is wrong, exit. Do not compound it.

**Key Quotes:**
- "The game of speculation is the most uniformly fascinating game in the world. But it is not a game for the stupid."
- "It never was my thinking that made the big money for me. It was always my sitting."
- "Experience proves that the market is always right, and that he who ignores or disregards what it says is foolish."

## 7. THE LEGACY — Purpose Beyond Profit + Human Side

Jesse Livermore died on November 28, 1940. He was 63.

### Reminiscences of a Stock Operator

In 1923, Edwin Lefèvre published "Reminiscences of a Stock Operator." The book is a fictionalized biography of Livermore. It is still read by traders today. The lessons are timeless.

### Failure

Livermore's story is not only about success. It is about the difficulty of maintaining discipline. He made fortunes. He lost them. He could not control himself outside of trading. He gambled. He drank. He took risks.

### What does he regret?

Livermore wrote rules. He violated them. In his later years, he expressed regret that he had not followed his own advice. He said: "I did precisely the wrong thing. I made money when I was right. I lost money when I was wrong. But I kept on making mistakes."

### What does he want to be remembered for?

Livermore did not speak explicitly about how he wanted to be remembered. But his rules have survived. Traders still study them. He is remembered as a cautionary tale—a genius who could not control himself.

## 8. FINAL MOTIVATION & PERSONAL APPLICATION Guide

Jesse Livermore's career demonstrates that trading skill without personal discipline is a path to ruin. He knew the rules. He could not follow them.

**5 Actionable Steps This Week:**

1. **Cut losses** — Livermore's rule: if a trade goes against you, get out. Do not hope. Do not wait. Get out.

2. **Let profits run** — Livermore's rule: do not exit too early. If you are right, sit. Do not cut winners.

3. **Never average down** — If a position is losing, do not add to it. You are already wrong.

4. **Follow your rules** — Livermore violated his own rules. That was his undoing. If you have rules, follow them.

5. **Control yourself** — Livermore gambled outside of trading. He drank. He could not control his personal life. Discipline in one area is not discipline in all areas.

**The Hard Question:**

If the greatest trader of his era could make and lose multiple fortunes because he could not control himself—what is your lack of discipline costing you?

The rules are easy. Following them is hard.
