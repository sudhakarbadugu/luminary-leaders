# Steve Jobs
## The Visionary Who Reimagined Technology

**Born:** February 24, 1955 — San Francisco, California
**Died:** October 5, 2011 — Palo Alto, California
**Net Worth:** ~$10 billion (at death)
**Key Companies:** Apple (cofounder), Pixar, NeXT

---

## Adoption and Early Childhood

Steven Paul Jobs was born in San Francisco to Abdandoned students
Joann Schieble and Gebre Hadis, an Ethiopian graduate student,
and was placed for adoption at birth.
He was adopted by Paul and Clara Jobs,
a working-class couple from Mountain View, California.
His adoptive father, Paul, worked as a machinist
and later became a repossessor, while his mother stayed home
to raise the family.
They lived in a modest house in a working-class neighborhood,
surrounded by other families who worked in manufacturing
and technology.

Growing up in Silicon Valley, young Steve was surrounded
by technology companies and the culture of engineering
that defined the region.
His father, Paul, taught him the fundamentals of electronics,
showing him how to disassemble and rebuild devices.
He instilled a sense of craftsmanship and attention to detail
that would define Jobs' approach to product design
for the rest of his life.
The message was clear.
Quality matters, even in things nobody sees.
The circuits inside the device, the screws beneath the surface.
Paul would often say that a good carpenter never uses bad wood,
and Steve remembered.

He attended Homestead High School in Cupertino,
where he excelled academically but was known as a loner.
He was more interested in English and photography
than chemistry and physics,
though he did take electronics class where he built Heathkits,
learning how electronic components worked together.
After graduation, he enrolled at Reed College in Oregon
but dropped out after one semester,
supporting himself with odd jobs and sleeping on friends' floors.
He would later say this was one of the best decisions he ever made,
freeing him to take classes purely out of interest
rather than obligation.
He sat in on classes that interested him, like calligraphy,
which later influenced Apple's typography.

## Atari Years and the Birth of Apple

In 1974, Jobs returned to California and joined Atari
as a video game designer.
His time at Atari was formative but short-lived.
He worked on the arcade game "Breakout" and clashed with colleagues
over work ethic and personal habits.
He was known for driving teammates crazy with his perfectionism,
often insisting designs be simplified beyond what others
thought reasonable.
He had strong opinions about the user experience,
arguing that games needed to be intuitive and fun,
not just technically impressive.

The experience taught him valuable lessons about product design
and user experience.
Atari's games were technically impressive
but often confusing to play.
Jobs understood that products needed to be intuitive, not just powerful.
They needed to feel natural from the first moment.
This insight would later inform his philosophy at Apple,
where he insisted on simplicity above all else.
He believed that the best products felt like they had always existed,
as if they were obvious choices.

In 1975, Jobs became obsessed with the Homebrew Computer Club,
where hobbyists gathered to share ideas about personal computing.
He saw the personal computer as a tool for empowerment.
Something that could give ordinary people access
to computational power previously available only to governments
and corporations.
He was convinced that the personal computer would change the world,
democratizing information in ways that had never happened before.

In 1976, Jobs and his friend Stephen Wozniak created the Apple I
computer in Jobs' parents' garage.
Jobs handled business negotiations and marketing
while Wozniak focused on engineering.
The machine sold for $666.66 and was the first pre-assembled
personal computer, though it still required a keyboard
and monitor to function.
The venture was modest but represented the beginning of a revolution.
They named their company Apple,
partly because Jobs had just returned from an apple farm
and liked the word's simplicity.

## Apple II and the Rise to Prominence

The Apple II, released in 1977, became a massive commercial success
and established Apple as a major player in the emerging
personal computer industry.
Unlike the Apple I, which required technical knowledge to operate,
the Apple II was designed for ease of use.
It featured color graphics, sound capability,
and an expandable architecture that allowed users
to customize their machines.
The product transformed Apple from a garage startup
into a serious competitor.

Apple's revenue jumped from $2.5 million in 1978
to $335 million in 1980, the year the company went public.
The IPO made 26-year-old Jobs a multimillionaire on paper
and transformed him into a public figure.
He was celebrated as a wunderkind.
The college dropout who had revolutionized an industry
before turning 30.
Magazine covers featured his face,
and business schools studied his career as a case study
in entrepreneurial success.

The Macintosh, launched in 1984,
introduced the graphical user interface (GUI) to the mass market.
Jobs had visited Xerox PARC and seen the Alto's GUI,
then hired away its creators to build the Mac.
The mouse-driven interface revolutionized computing,
setting the standard that Microsoft later copied with Windows.
The famous "1984" Super Bowl commercial positioned the Mac
as a tool for creative rebels, not corporate drones.
It was directed by Ridley Scott
and remains one of the most iconic advertisements in history.

## Ouster and the NeXT Years

Jealousy and boardroom politics led to Jobs' removal from Apple
in 1985.
John Sculley, whom Jobs had recruited as CEO with the promise
of revitalizing Apple, orchestrated a board vote to strip him
of power.
Jobs was given a ceremonial title with no real authority
and eventually resigned, selling all but one share of his
Apple stock.
He had been betrayed by someone he had brought into the company,
and the experience wounded him deeply.

The ouster devastated Jobs emotionally.
He spent months wandering through Europe,
trying to understand what had gone wrong.
He concluded that he had been too demanding,
too abrasive, and too unwilling to compromise.
He emerged from the experience with a new perspective on leadership.
One that would inform his later success.
He learned that vision required patience,
that persuasion was more effective than command.

He founded NeXT Computer in 1986,
focusing on advanced workstations for education and business.
NeXT never achieved commercial success in terms of sales,
but its object-oriented operating system caught the attention
of researchers.
The company was ahead of its time,
developing technologies that would later become foundational
to the internet age.
Its operating system would eventually power Apple's resurrection.

## Pixar and the Return to Apple

In 1986, Jobs purchased the Graphics Group from Lucasfilm
for $5 million, renaming it Pixar.
He invested millions into developing rendering technology
and sought partnerships with Disney for animated films.
Pixar's first film, "Toy Story" (1995), became a blockbuster
and sparked a decade of iconic films including "Finding Nemo,"
"The Incredibles," and "Up."
The success transformed Pixar from a struggling technology company
into a cultural phenomenon.

Jobs negotiated the 2006 acquisition of Pixar by Disney
for $7.4 billion, making him Disney's largest shareholder
and cementing his status as one of entertainment's most powerful
figures.
He retained a seat on Disney's board until his death,
contributing to strategic decisions while respecting
the creative leadership already in place.
The acquisition validated his belief that technology and storytelling
could combine to create extraordinary experiences.

At Apple, Jobs returned in 1996 when Apple purchased NeXT
for $429 million.
He became interim CEO, then permanent CEO in 2001.
He took over a company in crisis and transformed it
into the most valuable company in the world.
He killed unsuccessful product lines, focused on a handful
of priorities, and insisted on design excellence
at every level.
His product reviews were legendary.
He would reject designs repeatedly until they achieved
the quality he demanded.

## The iEra: iPod, iPhone, and iPad

Jobs launched the iPod in 2001, revolutionizing the music industry.
The device was simple, elegant, and worked seamlessly
with Apple's iTunes software.
Within three years, the iPod dominated the portable music market
and iTunes became the world's largest music retailer.
Jobs had created an ecosystem that combined hardware, software,
and services in ways competitors had not anticipated.
The iPod proved that Apple could win in consumer electronics.

The iPhone, launched in 2007, transformed the telecommunications
industry.
Jobs insisted on a device that combined a phone, an iPod,
and an internet browser.
Three devices in one.
The touchscreen interface, the App Store,
and the seamless integration with iTunes created a new category
that competitors struggled to match.
By 2015, iPhone sales had generated over $1 trillion in revenue
for Apple, making it the most successful product
in corporate history.

The iPad, launched in 2010, created the tablet computing category.
Despite initial skepticism from analysts who thought the device
was just a larger iPhone, the iPad became the fastest-selling
consumer electronics product in history.
It transformed how people consumed media, worked, and communicated.
The iPad proved that the tablet form factor could replace laptops
for many tasks.

## Health Struggles and Death

In 2003, Jobs was diagnosed with a rare form of pancreatic
neuroendocrine tumor.
He spent nine years fighting the disease while continuing to lead
Apple.
He underwent surgery in 2004 and later liver transplants,
but his health declined steadily.
He stepped down as CEO in August 2011,
handing the reins to Tim Cook.
He died six weeks later, on October 5, 2011, at age 56.
His final public appearance was at the iPad 2 launch in March 2011,
where he appeared visibly frail but still commanded the stage.

Jobs left behind an extraordinary legacy.
Four trillion dollars in shareholder value.
The world's most valuable brand.
Products that redefined how humanity interacts with technology.
His uncompromising standards and relentless pursuit of excellence
remain Apple's foundation.
His memorial service at Stanford was attended by thousands,
including many of the world's most influential business
and technology leaders.
His influence extends beyond Apple.
He changed how we think about design, about product development,
about the relationship between technology and humanity.
