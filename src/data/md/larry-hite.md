# Larry Hite — The Trend Follower Who Survived by Limiting Risk

## 1. THE HOOK

He did not try to get rich. He tried not to get wiped out. While other traders bet their entire funds on single positions, Larry Hite had a rule: never risk more than 1% of capital on a single trade. While other traders sought the highest returns, Hite sought survival. In the 1987 crash, when the market fell 22% in one day, Hite's fund was profitable. Over decades, his systematic trend following produced returns that compounded. He did not swing for the fences. He did not have to. He survived while others died, and the compounding did the rest.

How does a trader who never bets more than 1% on a single position build wealth over decades?

## 2. ORIGIN — The "Before" (The Relatability Engine)

Lawrence D. Hite began trading in the 1970s. He was not a famous hedge fund manager. He was a trader who wanted to survive.

He worked with Richard Dennis and the Turtle Traders—though Hite was not one of the original Turtles. He was part of the broader community of systematic trend followers who believed that markets could be traded with rules, not discretion.

In the 1980s, Hite founded Mint Research, later Mint Investment Management. The firm used systematic trend following across diversified markets. The strategy was not novel—many others were doing similar things. Hite's contribution was the emphasis on risk management.

He was running from the fate of traders who got wiped out. He had seen traders bet too big, lose everything, and disappear. He wanted to stay in the game.

## 3. THE FIRE — Failures, Rock-Bottom & Turning Points

### Mint Investment Management

Mint Investment Management was a systematic trend following fund. It traded futures, currencies, commodities, and other markets. The rules were simple: follow trends, cut losses, let profits run, diversify.

But Hite's rule was different from other trend followers. He never risked more than 1% of capital on a single trade. If the trade went against him, the maximum loss was 1%. If the trade was a winner, he let it run.

### The 1987 Crash

On October 19, 1987, the S&P 500 fell 22% in a single day. Many traders were wiped out. Many hedge funds lost large percentages of their capital. Mint Investment Management was profitable that day. Hite's risk management had worked. The fund had diversified across markets. The positions that were losing were small.

### Low Volatility

Hite's returns were not volatile. He sought steady returns with low drawdowns. Other trend followers had larger drawdowns but larger peaks. Hite's approach was smoother. The compounding was steady.

## 4. THE GRIND — The Invisible Years (The Method)

Hite's method is defined by risk management, diversification, and systematic rules.

### Risk Management

Hite's core rule: never risk more than 1% of capital on a single trade. This rule prevents catastrophic losses. If a trade goes to zero, you lose 1%. If you are wrong ten times in a row, you lose approximately 10%. You survive.

### Diversification

Trend followers trade multiple markets: commodities, currencies, interest rates, stock indices. If one market is mean-reverting, another may be trending. Diversification reduces the probability that all positions lose at once.

### Systematic Rules

Hite did not trade discretionally. He had rules. The rules said when to buy, when to sell, how much to risk. The rules removed emotion. When the rules said buy, he bought. When they said sell, he sold.

The invisible years— the years before Mint—were years of learning what worked. Hite had seen traders who bet too big and got wiped out. He decided not to be one of them.

## 5. THE TEST — The Breakthrough Moment

The 1987 crash was the test. Many traders went out of business. Mint was profitable. The risk management had worked. The diversification had worked. The systematic rules had worked.

## 6. THE PHILOSOPHY — Stealable Wisdom

**On Survival:**
"I have survived while others have died because I limited my risk." Hite understood that survival is more important than profit. If you survive, you can trade tomorrow. If you are wiped out, you are done.

**On Being Wrong:**
"You don't have to be right, you just have to not be wrong too much." Hite's approach was not about predicting correctly. It was about limiting losses when wrong.

**On Position Sizing:**
Never risk more than 1% on a single trade. This rule enforces survival. Most traders fail because they bet too large.

## 7. THE LEGACY — Purpose Beyond Profit + Human Side

Larry Hite is less famous than other trend followers like Paul Tudor Jones or Bill Dunn. He did not have the largest returns or the largest fund. But he had a long track record with low volatility.

### What does he regret?

Hite has not spoken extensively about regrets. His approach avoided catastrophic losses. It is possible that his conservative position sizing reduced his returns relative to traders who bet larger. But his approach also kept him in the game.

### What does he want to be remembered for?

In interviews, Hite has said he wants to be remembered for demonstrating that survival is more important than profit. The traders who get rich are not always the ones who take the largest risks. Sometimes they are the ones who avoid being wiped out.

## 8. FINAL MOTIVATION & PERSONAL APPLICATION Guide

Larry Hite's career demonstrates that survival matters more than return. If you are wiped out, you cannot compound.

**5 Actionable Steps This Week:**

1. **Limit risk per trade** — Hite's rule: never risk more than 1% on a single position. If the trade goes against you, you lose 1%. You survive.

2. **Diversify** — Trade multiple markets. If one market is mean-reverting, another may be trending. Diversification reduces your probability of catastrophic loss.

3. **Do not be wrong too much** — You do not have to be right on every trade. You have to avoid being wrong so much that you are wiped out. Small losses are tolerable. Large losses are fatal.

4. **Follow your rules** — Hite's rules said when to buy and sell. He followed them. If you have rules, the key is following them, not making exceptions.

5. **Focus on survival** — If you survive, you can trade tomorrow. If you do not survive, the discussion about returns is over. Survival first. Returns second.

**The Hard Question:**

If the trader who never risked more than 1% on a single position was profitable on the day the market fell 22%—what size are your positions?

You do not have to be right. You have to not be wrong too much.
