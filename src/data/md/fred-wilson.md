# Fred Wilson

**Cofounder of Union Square Ventures, Early Twitter Investor**

---

## Section 1: The Venture Capitalist's Credo

Fred Wilson's impact on Silicon Valley cannot be measured solely in returns—though his fund Union Square Ventures has generated well over $1 billion in value—but in the cultural philosophy he brought to venture capital, one that emphasized product quality, user engagement, and long-term thinking over quick exits and short-term metrics. Wilson's investment thesis, articulated in a famous blog post titled "The Venture Capitalist's Credo," argued that the best startups solved real problems for real users, that founders should resist pressure to grow too fast, and that the best companies were built for the decades-long horizon rather than the next quarterly report.

The manifesto resonated because it articulated what many founders believed but rarely heard from investors: that sustainable value creation required patience, that the market's short-term demands were often contrary to a company's long-term health, and that venture capital could be a force for good in the industry if practiced thoughtfully. The Credo became required reading in startup circles, and its influence extended beyond individual investments to shape how founders thought about the relationship between themselves and their investors. Wilson had defined a set of principles that distinguished him from the transaction-oriented investors who dominated the industry.

## Section 2: Early Career and Education

Wilson was born in 1961 in New York City, the son of a physicist and a schoolteacher, and grew up in a family that valued intellectual achievement over financial success. He studied at MIT, earning degrees in electrical engineering and computer science, and began his career at a series of technology companies before transitioning to investment management. He worked at Benchmark Capital and later at Flatiron Partners, where he invested in early internet companies through the 1990s, learning firsthand the patterns that distinguished enduring businesses from temporary phenomena.

The dot-com boom and bust taught him the dangers of speculation over substance. He saw too many companies built on hype rather than genuine value creation, and he saw what happened to investors who poured money into concepts without business models. The lessons would inform his approach for the rest of his career: he would never again invest in something he didn't understand, no matter how hot the market was. He would never pressure founders to grow faster than their businesses could sustain. He would never confuse a rising stock price with a functioning company.

## Section 3: Union Square Ventures and the Twitter Bet

In 2004, Wilson co-founded Union Square Ventures with Brad Burnham, betting that the next wave of transformative companies would be built around the internet's social and mobile capabilities. Their early investments included Twitter, whose 2006 seed round they led, producing one of the most profitable venture returns in history—early estimates suggested returns in the hundreds of millions of dollars. Wilson's investment in Twitter was characteristic of his approach: he saw a simple product that solved a real communication problem, he trusted the founders' judgment about how to develop it, and he resisted pressure to push for monetization before the product had achieved critical mass.

Twitter's long journey to profitability validated his patience, even as critics argued that the company would never figure out how to make money. The timeline was longer than any investor wanted, but Wilson understood something that many of his peers didn't: that social networks have fundamental network effects that make early dominance extremely valuable, and that monetization could be optimized once the user base was established. The bet was not without risk—Twitter nearly imploded multiple times—but the fundamental insight proved correct.

## Section 4: Investment Philosophy and Portfolio

Union Square Ventures' portfolio included Foursquare, Zynga, Tumblr, Coinbase, DocuSign, and Kickstarter—companies that reflected Wilson's interest in platforms that enabled user-generated content and social interaction. He was known for taking contrarian positions, investing in categories other VCs avoided, and maintaining relationships with founders that extended well beyond typical board interactions. The relationships were not merely professional: Wilson genuinely liked the founders he backed, and he invested in people as much as ideas, believing that the best founders could navigate circumstances that would defeat lesser ones.

His blog, "AVC.com," became one of the most widely read resources for entrepreneurs seeking honest, unvarnished advice about building companies. Wilson wrote openly about failure—he has admitted to investing in companies that went nowhere—and about the importance of product-market fit. He wrote about the dangers of raising too much money, about the value of being a learning organization, and about the importance of focusing on what matters. The writing was not marketing: it was an extension of his investment philosophy, made public because he believed the lessons were too valuable to keep private.

## Section 5: The Openness Philosophy

Wilson was an early advocate for open platforms, arguing that startups benefited more from building on open infrastructure than from joining closed ecosystems. He opposed the trend toward proprietary platforms that locked in developers and users, and he invested in companies that promoted openness as a business model. This philosophy extended to his writing, which was consistently transparent about his investment theses, his portfolio's performance, and his views on where the industry was heading. He was one of the first major VCs to publish his fund returns publicly, a practice that was unusual in an industry that valued confidentiality above all.

The openness was both philosophical and practical: Wilson believed that transparency built trust and that trust was the foundation of effective partnerships between investors and founders. The alternative—relationships based on information asymmetry and strategic manipulation—produced transactions but not lasting partnerships. He wanted to be the kind of investor that founders would recommend to their friends, and that required being honest even when honesty was uncomfortable. The approach was more sustainable than the alternative, even if it occasionally cost him a deal.

## Section 6: The Social Media Era and After

Wilson's career spanned the transformation of the internet from a primarily content-based medium to a social one. He invested early in companies that understood that the value of the internet came not from its infrastructure but from the people who used it—that networks of individuals communicating with each other were more powerful than broadcast media delivered to passive consumers. This insight was not unique to him, but his willingness to act on it consistently, across multiple companies and multiple market cycles, distinguished him from investors who saw the trend but hesitated to commit.

The subsequent evolution of social media—from Twitter's text-based brevity to Instagram's visual emphasis to TikTok's algorithmic video—confirmed his view that the social internet would continue to transform how people communicate, create, and consume information. His investments in platforms that enabled these transformations reflected a conviction that the social internet was still in its early stages, with much more transformation to come. Even as concerns about social media's negative effects grew, Wilson maintained that the net impact of connecting billions of people was profoundly positive.

## Section 7: Personal Life and Continuing Influence

Wilson is married to his wife Christy, has two adult children, and lives in Manhattan where he is active in local civic matters. He serves on several nonprofit boards and has been an outspoken advocate for making venture capital more accessible to underrepresented founders. His annual letters to founders, published openly on his blog, have become required reading for the startup community, offering clear-eyed analysis of market conditions and strategic guidance that reflects decades of pattern recognition. The letters are not optimistic cheerleading; they are honest assessments of what founders should expect and how they should prepare.

His belief that technology serves humanity best when it is built with patience and purpose has made him one of the most respected voices in an industry often criticized for short-term thinking. The questions he has always asked—from the earliest days of his career through every market cycle—remain the right ones: Does this product solve a real problem? Does the team have the grit to see it through? Is this someone we would want to work with for decades? These questions, applied consistently, have produced a portfolio of companies that have created billions of dollars in value while also contributing something more durable: a model for how venture capital should be practiced.

## Section 8: Legacy and Enduring Principles

Wilson's legacy will be defined by the companies he backed and the culture he created around his investment approach. The returns are important but secondary: they are evidence that his approach works, not the reason for it. The reason for it—the genuine desire to back founders who are solving real problems, the willingness to be patient, the commitment to transparency—will endure as the most significant part of his contribution. Future investors will look back at his example and see that it is possible to be both successful and principled, that the two are not mutually exclusive.

The enduring principles from his career are clear: focus on real problems, trust good founders, be patient, and operate with transparency. These principles are not unique to Wilson, but his consistent application of them over decades provides a template that others can follow. The venture capital industry has changed dramatically since he started, and it will change just as dramatically in the decades to come. But the fundamental insights about what makes companies valuable and what makes partnerships work will remain relevant long after the specific investments have been forgotten.
