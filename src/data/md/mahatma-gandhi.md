# Mahatma Gandhi

**Leader of Indian Independence | Pioneer of Nonviolent Resistance | 1869–1948**

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi, universally known as Mahatma (Great Soul), led India to independence from British colonial rule through a campaign of nonviolent civil disobedience that would inspire civil rights movements worldwide. His philosophy of satyagraha (truth-force) and his willingness to suffer for his principles transformed the nature of political resistance and established nonviolent resistance as a powerful tool for social change.

## Early Life and Education in India

Mohandas Gandhi was born on October 2, 1869, in Porbandar, a coastal town in what is now Gujarat, India. His father served as the chief minister of the small princely state. Gandhi married Kasturba Makhanji at age thirteen, a marriage arrangement typical of the time. He completed his matriculation in Bombay and, against family expectations, agreed to travel to London in 1888 to study law at the Inner Temple. He struggled initially with the English educational system and with adhering to vegetarianism (a vow he had made to his mother), but eventually adapted to London society and was called to the bar in 1891. He returned to India, practiced law briefly, and in 1893 accepted a position with a commercial firm in South Africa.

## South Africa and the Birth of Resistance Leader

Gandhi spent over twenty years in South Africa, where he experienced systematic racial discrimination firsthand. A defining incident occurred when he was thrown off a train for refusing to move from a first-class compartment despite holding a valid ticket. This discrimination, combined with broader segregation policies, transformed him from a quiet professional into a political activist. He founded the Natal Indian Congress in 1894 and organized petitions, strikes, and boycotts of British goods. He developed the philosophy of satyagraha—holding firmly to truth—and organized non-violent protests against discriminatory laws, receiving multiple prison sentences that hardened his commitment to nonviolent resistance.

## Return to India and Freedom Movement

Gandhi returned to India in 1915 and quickly became the leading figure in the Indian National Congress, the main organization fighting British rule. He transformed the independence movement from an elite political negotiation into a mass mobilization campaign using indigenous cultural symbols, simple living, and village engagement. He led the Champaran and Kheda movements in 1918, experimenting with nonviolent resistance against exploitative taxation. The Rowlatt Act protests in 1919, during which British soldiers killed hundreds of unarmed civilians, marked a turning point—Gandhi called for nationwide hartal (strike) and was imprisoned for the first time, launching decades of intermittent imprisonment.

## Salt March and Civil Disobedience

In 1930, Gandhi undertook his most famous single action: the Salt March. Walking 241 miles over twenty-four days to the coastal village of Dandi, he and thousands of followers made salt from seawater in defiance of British monopoly—a direct violation of the law that affected every Indian household. The march captured international attention and sparked similar local actions across India. Over 60,000 Indians were imprisoned for making salt or buying contraband foreign cloth. Gandhi was imprisoned but released in 1931 to negotiate with the British Viceroy, representing the growing political strength of India's independence movement.

## Quit India and Final Years

In 1942, Gandhi launched the "Quit India" movement, demanding immediate British withdrawal from India. He was imprisoned for most of the next three years. British officials agreed to grant India independence in 1947, though the partition of British India into India and Pakistan—division along religious lines—devastated Gandhi. He opposed partition but accepted it as the only way to prevent mass civil war. He worked to restore peace between Hindus and Muslims, undertaking fasts and mediation efforts, alienating some Hindus who blamed him for accepting partition. On January 30, 1948, Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist, assassinated Gandhi in New Delhi.

## Legacy of Nonviolent Resistance

Gandhi's philosophy of nonviolent resistance—ahimsa combined with satyagraha—influenced Martin Luther King Jr., Nelson Mandela, Aung San Suu Kyi, and countless other activists worldwide. His methods demonstrated that oppressed peoples could resist without adopting the violent tactics of their oppressors, preserving moral authority and often winning broader public support. His vision of swadeshi (self-sufficiency), village governance, and simple living shaped development discourse beyond politics. India achieved independence, though partition caused enormous suffering, and Gandhi's legacy remains contested—celebrated globally as a prophet of peace while criticized by some Indians who blame him for accepting the partition of their subcontinent.
