# John Paulson — The Man Who Saw the Housing Crash and Bet Everything

## 1. THE HOOK

In 2005, he saw something that almost no one else saw. The housing market was booming. Mortgage-backed securities were rated AAA. Banks were lending to borrowers with no income, no assets, no documentation. Paulson believed it was a bubble. He could not find anyone to agree with him. The big banks thought subprime mortgages were safe. The ratings agencies said they were investment grade. Paulson shorted them anyway. When the market crashed in 2008, his fund made approximately $15-20 billion. It was called "The Greatest Trade Ever." He was right. But his later trades were not as successful.

How does a man make the trade of a lifetime and then struggle to repeat it?

## 2. ORIGIN — The "Before" (The Relatability Engine)

John Alfred Paulson was born December 14, 1955, in Queens, New York. His father was a CFO of a publishing company. The family was middle class.

Paulson studied finance at New York University, graduating in 1978. He then earned an MBA from Harvard Business School in 1980. He was interested in investing from a young age.

He worked at Bear Stearns and then at Odyssey Partners, a hedge fund run by Leon Levy. In 1994, he founded Paulson & Co., his own hedge fund.

He was running from mediocrity. He wanted to manage money. But his early years were not extraordinary. He was not known as a famous investor. He was a merger arbitrage specialist—buying companies that were being acquired, shorting the acquirers, and capturing the spread.

## 3. THE FIRE — Failures, Rock-Bottom & Turning Points

### The Housing Trade (2005-2007)

In 2005, Paulson began researching the housing market. He noticed that mortgage lending standards had collapsed. Borrowers were getting loans with no income verification. Subprime mortgages were being packaged into complex securities called CDOs (collateralized debt obligations). The ratings agencies gave them AAA ratings.

Paulson believed that the AAA ratings were wrong. He believed that many of these mortgages would default. He bought credit default swaps on CDOs—essentially insurance that would pay out if the bonds defaulted.

The trade cost money. Every month, he paid premiums on the swaps. The housing market kept rising. Investors in his fund questioned the trade. Paulson persisted.

In 2007, the market turned. Subprime mortgages defaulted. The CDOs collapsed. Paulson's fund made approximately $15-20 billion. It was one of the largest profits in hedge fund history.

### The Gold Trade (2009-)

After the housing trade, Paulson became bullish on gold. He believed that inflation would rise as governments printed money to fight the financial crisis. He bought large positions in the SPDR Gold Trust and in gold miners. Gold rose. The trade was profitable. But it did not match the housing trade.

### European Sovereign Debt (2011-2012)

Paulson bet on European sovereign debt. He believed that the European debt crisis would resolve favorably. The trade lost money. The Advantage funds declined.

### Valeant (2015)

Paulson invested in Valeant Pharmaceuticals, a drug company. The stock collapsed amid scandals about pricing and accounting. Paulson's fund lost billions on the position.

### Decline

After 2011, Paulson's funds underperformed. Investors withdrew. Assets under management declined. His net worth dropped from a peak of approximately $18 billion to approximately $4 billion.

## 4. THE GRIND — The Invisible Years (The Method)

Paulson's method is defined by concentrated bets and deep research.

### Deep Research

The housing trade was not a guess. Paulson researched mortgage lending. He read the prospectuses of mortgage-backed securities. He understood the structure of CDOs. He did not trust the ratings agencies. He did his own analysis.

### Concentrated Bets

Paulson does not diversify. He takes large positions in things he believes in. The housing trade was a concentrated bet. The gold trade was a concentrated bet. Concentration can produce large returns. It can also produce large losses.

### Risk

The risk of the housing trade was not small. Paulson paid premiums on credit default swaps for two years before the trade paid off. If the housing market had not crashed, the premiums would have accumulated. The bet was not free.

The invisible years— the years before the housing trade—were years of merger arbitrage. Paulson was a competent investor. He was not famous. The housing trade made him famous.

## 5. THE TEST — The Breakthrough Moment

The 2007-2008 housing trade was the test. Paulson was right when almost everyone else was wrong. The trade made billions. Gregory Zuckerman wrote a book about it: "The Greatest Trade Ever."

But the test continued after the trade. Could Paulson repeat his success? The answer, so far, is that he has not.

## 6. THE PHILOSOPHY — Stealable Wisdom

**On the Housing Trade:**
"I don't think we'll see another opportunity like this in our lifetime." Paulson believed the housing bubble was a once-in-a-generation event. The concentration of mispricing and the scale of the market made it possible to bet billions.

**On Concentration:**
Paulson takes concentrated positions. He does not spread his bets across hundreds of securities. He bets heavily on what he believes in.

**On Being Early:**
Paulson began shorting housing in 2005. The market did not crash until 2007. For two years, he paid premiums. Being early is expensive.

**Key Quotes:**
- "I don't think we'll see another opportunity like this in our lifetime."
- "We were correct in our analysis."

## 7. THE LEGACY — Purpose Beyond Profit + Human Side

John Paulson is still active. His fund manages money, though with fewer assets than at its peak.

### Wealth

Paulson's net worth peaked at approximately $18 billion. It has since declined to approximately $4 billion. He is still wealthy. He is no longer one of the richest people in the world.

### Personal Life

Paulson has been married and divorced. He has children. He has donated to philanthropic causes.

### What does he regret?

Paulson has said that some of his later trades were mistakes. The Valeant investment is an example. He has also acknowledged that the years after the housing trade were difficult.

### What does he want to be remembered for?

In interviews, Paulson has said he wants to be remembered for protecting investors during the financial crisis. The housing trade did that. But the years after tested his reputation.

## 8. FINAL MOTIVATION & PERSONAL APPLICATION Guide

John Paulson's career demonstrates that one trade can define a career. He made billions shorting housing. He has struggled to repeat it.

**5 Actionable Steps This Week:**

1. **Do your own analysis** — Paulson did not trust the ratings agencies. He did his own analysis of mortgage-backed securities. If you are relying on consensus ratings, you are not doing your own work.

2. **Be willing to pay to wait** — Paulson paid premiums on CDS for two years before the trade paid off. Being right is expensive if you are early.

3. **Concentrated bets are risky** — Paulson made concentrated bets that paid off. He also made concentrated bets that lost. Concentration can make you. It can break you.

4. **One trade does not make a career** — The housing trade made Paulson famous. The years after tested his reputation. If you have one great trade, you are not done.

5. **Do not repeat what worked** — The housing trade was successful because the market was broken in a specific way. That exact opportunity will not come again. Look for mispricings, but do not assume that the next one will be the same.

**The Hard Question:**

If one trade can define a career—what trade are you avoiding because you do not want to pay to wait?

The largest profits come from being right when everyone else is wrong. But you pay for the right to be right.
