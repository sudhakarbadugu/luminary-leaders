# Marie Curie

**Nobel Prize in Physics & Chemistry | Discovery of Radium | First Woman to Win Nobel Prize**

Marie Curie (Maria Skłodowska-Curie) was a Polish-born physicist and chemist who conducted pioneering research on radioactivity, becoming the first woman to win a Nobel Prize and the only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences. Her discovery of radium and polonium, her pioneering radiological research, and her dedication to scientific truth despite enormous personal cost established her as one of history's greatest scientists.

## Early Life in Poland

Maria Skłodowska was born on November 7, 1867, in Warsaw, Poland, which was then under Russian imperial rule. She was the youngest of five children in an intellectual family—her father was a physics and mathematics teacher, her mother ran a prestigious girls' school. Education was paramount in the Skłodowski family, and Maria excelled academically from childhood. However, Poland's Russian rulers limited higher education opportunities for women, particularly in the sciences. Maria and her sister Bronya made a pact: Maria would work as a governess to fund Bronya's medical school in Paris, then Bronya would reciprocate. In 1891, at age twenty-four, Maria moved to Paris to study at the Sorbonne, changing her name to Marie and beginning the scientific career that would define her legacy.

## Scientific Education and Early Partnership

In Paris, Marie studied physics and mathematics at the Sorbonne, earning her physics degree in 1893 (first in her class) and mathematics degree in 1894. She met Pierre Curie in 1894, a French physicist studying magnetic properties of steel. They shared scientific interests and a passion for research that transcended social conventions. They married in 1895, with Marie declining a lucrative marriage offer to continue her research alongside Pierre. Their partnership combined Marie's rigorous experimental approach with Pierre's theoretical knowledge, producing one of science's most productive collaborations. In 1897, their daughter Irène was born, the first of two daughters who would both become Nobel laureates themselves.

## Discovery of Radioactivity

Building on Henri Becquerel's 1896 discovery of uranium radiation, Marie began investigating whether other substances emitted similar radiation. Using an electrometer invented by Pierre and Jacques Curie, she measured the radiation emitted by various chemical compounds and discovered that thorium also emitted similar rays. She coined the term "radioactivity" to describe this phenomenon. Most remarkably, her measurements showed that the intensity of radiation depended only on the quantity of uranium or thorium in the compound—not its chemical composition, suggesting that radioactivity was an atomic property. This insight, unprecedented in scientific history, established the study of atomic structure and would revolutionize physics and chemistry.

## Discovery of Polonium and Radium

In 1898, the Curies discovered two new chemical elements. Working with pitchblende ore, they detected an unknown substance emitting far more radiation than pure uranium. By systematically separating the pitchblende into components and measuring each's radioactivity, they isolated a new element—named polonium in honor of Marie's homeland Poland. Within the same year, they discovered a second element, radium, found in minute quantities after processing tons of pitchblende. Isolating pure radium required processing enormous quantities of ore in difficult conditions. Marie personally processed over a ton of pitchblende residue in a leaky shed, eventually extracting one-tenth of a gram of pure radium chloride in 1910—an effort that took four years.

## Nobel Prizes and Recognition

The Curies' work received immediate recognition. In 1903, Marie and Pierre shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Henri Becquerel for their research on radioactivity. Marie became the first woman to receive a Nobel Prize. Pierre died tragically in 1906, struck by a horse-drawn carriage while crossing a Paris street. Marie took over his teaching position at the Sorbonne, becoming the university's first female professor. In 1911, she received a second Nobel Prize in Chemistry for her discovery of polonium and radium, making her the first person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences. The prize money enabled her to build a properly equipped laboratory, finally establishing the research facility she had long lacked.

## World War I and Final Years

During World War I, Marie developed mobile X-ray units—"petites Curies"—that allowed battlefield surgeons to locate bullets and fractures in wounded soldiers, saving countless lives. She trained over 150 women as X-ray technicians and drove vehicles equipped with the equipment to field hospitals. The war exhausted her physically, and long-term exposure to radiation damaged her health. She continued research into radioactive isotopes and their medical applications, working to establish radioactivity as a tool for treating cancer. She died on July 4, 1934, from aplastic anemia likely caused by her radiation exposure. Her personal papers remain radioactive to this day and are stored in lead-lined boxes.

## Legacy and Scientific Impact

Marie Curie's scientific legacy is immense. Her discovery of radioactivity fundamentally changed understanding of matter and led directly to nuclear physics, medical imaging, cancer treatment, and atomic energy. Her work established that atoms were divisible—a revolutionary concept that led to twentieth-century physics. Her notebooks from the 1890s remain contaminated and are kept in lead-lined boxes at the Bibliothèque nationale de France, accessible only to researchers who agree to the radioactivity restrictions. She was the first to use radioactive isotopes for medical treatment and pioneered the understanding of radiation's biological effects. Her two daughters, Irène Joliot-Curie and Ève Curie, both became distinguished scientists and humanitarians, continuing the family's tradition of scientific excellence.
