# David Attenborough

**Broadcaster, Natural Historian, BBC Wildlife Documentarian**

## Early Life and Passion for Nature

David Attenborough was born in 1926 in Leicester, England, into a wealthy family that encouraged his fascination with wildlife. As a child, he collected fossils, minerals, and bird eggs, and spent hours observing the natural world in the English countryside. He attended Clare College, Cambridge, where he studied natural sciences with a focus on zoology. After graduation, he joined the Royal Navy and served on HMS Ambrosia, where he learned to film and edit footage — a skill that would transform his career.

## Joining the BBC

Attenborough joined the BBC in 1952 as a producer in the Talks department. He was assigned to the newly formed Television Talks section, where he helped create natural history programs. In 1954, he produced and narrated "Zoo Quest," a series that took viewers to exotic locations to film animals in the wild. The show was an immediate hit and established Attenborough as the BBC's leading wildlife presenter. He was appointed head of the BBC's Natural History Unit in 1965 but never stopped presenting.

## Planet Earth and the Natural History Revolution

Attenborough's career reached global prominence with the "Life" series of documentaries. He presented "Life on Earth" (1979), which traced the evolution of living organisms from the first cells to complex animals. The series was seen by over 500 million people worldwide and established the template for natural history television. He followed with "The Living Planet" (1984), "The Trials of Life" (1990), "The Private Life of Plants" (1995), and many others. Each series pushed the boundaries of wildlife filmmaking, using new technologies like macro photography and aerial cinematography.

## Planet Earth II and Modern Legacy

In 2016, Attenborough presented "Planet Earth II," a sequel to the 2006 original. The series featured breathtaking footage of animals in their habitats, including snow leopards in the Himalayas and marine iguanas in the Galápagos. It became the most-watched nature documentary in British television history. Attenborough was 90 years old when it aired, demonstrating remarkable stamina and continuing relevance. He followed it with "Dynasties" (2018), which focused on animal families and their struggles for survival, and "The Green Planet" (2022), exploring plant behavior.

## Seven Decades at the BBC

Attenborough spent nearly 70 years at the BBC, beginning as a producer and eventually becoming the world's most recognized naturalist. He served as controller of BBC Two from 1965 to 1969, where he commissioned landmark programs and expanded the channel's cultural reach. However, he gave up the administrative role to return to program-making, favoring creative work over management. His decision to step back from leadership made him one of the longest-serving broadcasters in BBC history.

## Documentary Technique and Narration Style

Attenborough was known for his distinctive, unhurried narration style that conveyed wonder without anthropomorphizing animals. He wrote all his own scripts, often traveling to film locations in difficult conditions. He was integral to the development of the "blue chip" documentary style — high production values, cinematic footage, and meticulous research. He pioneered the use of time-lapse photography, infrared cameras, and drone technology to capture animal behavior in ways never before seen on television.

## Environmental Advocacy

Attenborough used his platform to advocate for conservation and environmental protection. He spoke openly about climate change, biodiversity loss, and the urgent need to protect natural habitats. In 2019, he presented "Climate Change — The Facts," examining the science and consequences of global warming. He was a founding member of the WWF and served as a trustee for many conservation organizations. He was knighted by Queen Elizabeth II and received the Order of Merit, one of the highest honors in Britain.

## Legacy and Global Influence

Attenborough influenced generations of naturalists, filmmakers, and scientists. His documentaries inspired millions to care about the natural world and became standard viewing in schools worldwide. He received dozens of honorary degrees and awards, including the Crystal Award at the World Economic Forum. At 98, he remained active, filming new projects and advocating for planetary stewardship. He became the face of environmental consciousness for millions, using his authority and charm to make complex ecological issues accessible and urgent.
