# Stewart Butterfield — Slack Cofounder and Philosopher of Pivots

## 1. THE HOOK

In 2002, Stewart Butterfield built a game called Game Neverending. It failed commercially. But the internal team photo-sharing tool they built became Flickr—sold to Yahoo for $35 million. In 2009, he built another game called Glitch. It also failed. But again, the internal communication tool became Slack—sold to Salesforce for $27.7 billion in 2021. Two games failed. Two pivots. Two billion-dollar exits. He once said: "It's really hard to design products by focus groups. A lot of times, people don't know what they want until you show it to them."

How does a philosophy major from rural Canada turn repeated failure into tech empire?

## 2. ORIGIN — THE "BEFORE" (The Relatability Engine)

Stewart Butterfield was born March 21, 1973, in Morris, Manitoba, Canada—a small town of roughly 1,000 people. His father was a hog farmer. His mother was a secretary. There was no tech industry nearby. No venture capital. No Silicon Valley dreams.

He studied at the University of Victoria, earning a BA in Philosophy. He then went to Cambridge University for an MPhil in Philosophy, specializing in epistemology—the study of knowledge itself. Academic philosophy taught him to question assumptions and think deeply about problems before jumping to solutions.

After Cambridge, Butterfield moved to Vancouver. He worked at a web design company but dreamed of building something big. In 2002, he co-founded Ludicorp with Catero fake: [Catero and Eric.] The vision: build a massively multiplayer online game (MMO) called Game Neverending. The game featured user-generated content, virtual economies, and social features—a decade ahead of its time.

But Game Neverending never found an audience. The servers were expensive. The funding was running out. The game launched in 2004 but was already dying. By 2005, Ludicorp had to pivot or die.

## 3. THE FIRE — FAILURES, ROCK-BOTTOM & TURNING POINTS

### Ludicorp's Pivot to Flickr (2002-2005)

While building Game Neverending, the Ludicorp team needed a way to share screenshots internally. They built a quick photo-sharing tool. When the game failed, Butterfield looked at what had value. The photo-sharing tool—Flickr—was more promising than the game.

Flickr launched in 2004 with innovative features: tagging, groups, and a social layer. It was unlike anything else at the time. Users could organize photos with keywords and discover other photographers. The Yahoo acquisition in 2005 was reportedly around $35 million.

Butterfield left Yahoo in 2008. The Flickr experience taught him: internal tools built during failed projects often have more value than the original product.

### Tiny Speck and Glitch (2009-2012)

In 2009, Butterfield founded Tiny Speck in New York. The plan: build another game called Glitch—a whimsical, creative MMO. Glitch launched in 2011 but struggled to find players. The game closed in December 2012.

During development, Tiny Speck built an internal chat tool to communicate across remote teams. When Glitch failed, Butterfield looked at that internal tool again. History was repeating itself.

### The Birth of Slack (2012-2013)

Butterfield and his team rebuilt the internal communication tool. They added channels, direct messaging, and integrations. In 2013, Slack launched as a product. It was one of the fastest B2B growth stories in tech history.

Slack went public in 2019 via direct listing (avoiding traditional IPO). By 2021, Salesforce acquired Slack for $27.7 billion—the largest enterprise software acquisition at the time.

## 4. THE GRIND — THE INVISIBLE YEARS (THE METHOD)

### Game-First, Pivot to Product

Butterfield's pattern became clear: build a game. If it fails commercially, look at the tools created internally. Those tools—Flickr and Slack—became the success stories. The pivot was only possible because Butterfield stayed observant during failure.

### Philosophy-Driven Decisions

Butterfield's epistemology background shaped how he approached problems. He once explained in an interview: "Philosophy teaches you to think about what you actually know versus what you think you know. That's directly applicable to building products." He questioned assumptions, tested hypotheses, and moved slowly toward certainty.

### Authentic Culture Building

At Tiny Speck, Butterfield built a quirky internal culture. They had a Slack channel called "History of the World" where employees shared random facts. He believed software should feel human, not corporate. When Slack launched, this culture translated into product personality—channels had fun default names, emoji reactions were encouraged, and the interface avoided cold corporate aesthetics.

### Focus on Problem, Not Solution

Butterfield's famous quote is "Focus on the problem, not the solution." He applied this to hiring, product development, and company strategy. He believed most entrepreneurs start with a solution in mind and try to find a problem to fit. Instead, he started with problems and let solutions emerge.

## 5. THE TEST — THE BREAKTHROUGH MOMENT

The $27.7 billion Salesforce acquisition was the ultimate test. Slack transformed workplace communication. Over 20 million daily active users by 2021. Integration with thousands of apps. Enterprise customers like NASA, Target, and Shopify.

Butterfield stayed through the acquisition but left in 2023 to focus on other projects. His net worth from the sale exceeded $1 billion. The test proved: pivots built fortunes.

## 6. THE PHILOSOPHY — STEALABLE WISDOM

**On Product Design:**
"It's really hard to design products by focus groups. A lot of times, people don't know what they want until you show it to them."

**On Future:**
"The best way to predict the future is to invent it."

**On Failure:**
"Every pivot was born from failure. The key is to see the hidden value in what you've built."

## 7. THE LEGACY — PURPOSE BEYOND PROFIT + HUMAN SIDE

Stewart Butterfield left Slack after the acquisition. He became a private investor and advisor. He co-founded a new stealth startup in 2024.

He remains known for humble personality despite success. He credits his philosophy education for teaching him to think clearly. He has spoken about mental health in tech, emphasizing that entrepreneurs should prioritize well-being over endless hustle.

He wants to be remembered as someone who built tools that helped people collaborate better—not just as a tech entrepreneur.

## 8. FINAL MOTIVATION & PERSONAL APPLICATION GUIDE

**5 Actionable Steps This Week:**

1. **Pivot from failure, not around it** — Butterfield turned two failed games into Flickr and Slack. Look at your internal tools during difficult projects. Hidden value exists.

2. **Use philosophy as a decision framework** — Butterfield's epistemology training helped him distinguish what he actually knew from what he assumed. Question your assumptions this week.

3. **Build culture authentically** — "History of the World" channel wasn't corporate theater. Culture emerges from genuine values. Let yours emerge naturally.

4. **Don't rely on focus groups** — People don't know what they want until you show them. Build prototypes and show them. Learn from reactions.

5. **Invent the future** — Don't wait for opportunities. Create them. Build something and see who wants it.

**The Hard Question:**

If Butterfield turned two failed games into $27.7 billion—are you treating failure as an endpoint or a pivot point?

Pivots build fortunes. Failure is just the beginning.
