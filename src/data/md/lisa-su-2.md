# Lisa Su — AMD CEO

## 1. THE HOOK
In 2014, Lisa Su inherited an AMD that had lost $1 billion the previous year, was laying off 7% of its workforce, and had just replaced its CEO for the third time in six years. The company was commonly written off as a technical has-been, a maker of inferior chips that could never catch Intel. Eight years later, Su had turned AMD into the hottest chip company in the world: its market capitalization had grown from $3 billion to over $100 billion, its CPUs were powering the fastest computers on earth, and its data center revenue had grown tenfold. Her story is a case study in turnaround leadership and the power of technical conviction.

## 2. ORIGIN — THE "Before" (The Relatability Engine)
Lisa Su was born on November 7, 1969, in Taipei, Taiwan, the daughter of an engineer father who encouraged her interest in math and science. Her family moved to the United States when she was 3 years old, settling in Houston, Texas, where her father worked in the oil industry. She was a gifted student who excelled in mathematics, winning a national math competition in the 6th grade and earning a scholarship to MIT, where she earned bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees in electrical engineering. Her doctoral dissertation focused on copper interconnect technology, a specialty that positioned her perfectly for the semiconductor industry.

After MIT, Su worked at Texas Instruments, then moved to Sun Microsystems, where she worked on microprocessor design. She joined AMD in 2012 as a senior vice president, overseeing the company's semiconductor design and development. Her technical background was unusual for a CEO: she understood the details of chip architecture and manufacturing in a way that most of her predecessors at AMD had not. She was known for being hands-on, visiting fabs, reviewing chip designs, and engaging directly with engineers about technical decisions. This credibility with the technical community became a key asset when she took over as CEO.

## 3. THE FIRE — Failures, Rock-Bottom & Turning Points
Su's turnaround strategy required making painful decisions early. When she took over as CEO, AMD was spread across too many product lines and customer segments, trying to compete with Intel in servers while also chasing Nvidia in graphics. Su made the counter-intuitive decision to exit the server market — the most prestigious and profitable segment — to focus resources on a smaller number of higher-impact products. The decision was controversial: analysts and employees questioned whether AMD could survive without a server presence, but Su believed that competing with Intel head-on before AMD's technology was ready would be fatal.

The turning point came in 2017 when AMD launched its Ryzen processor line, which was the first AMD product in years that could match or exceed Intel's comparable offerings in performance. The launch was the result of Su's technical leadership: she had insisted on a new chip architecture that prioritized single-thread performance, a critical metric for consumer and gaming applications. The Ryzen launch exceeded expectations and marked the beginning of AMD's recovery. Su used the momentum to rebuild confidence in the company, telling employees that AMD would not try to be all things to all people, but would focus on being the best in the segments it chose to compete in.

## 4. THE GRIND — The Invisible Years (The Method)
Su's management philosophy emphasized "operational intensity" — a term she borrowed from her MIT training that describes the discipline of managing complex processes with precision. She introduced weekly operational reviews at AMD, tracking yield rates, inventory levels, and customer demand with the rigor of a manufacturing executive. She built a culture of accountability where missing targets was discussed openly and corrective actions were implemented quickly. She also invested heavily in customer relationships, personally visiting key accounts like Microsoft and Sony to understand their needs and ensure AMD was designing products that met them.

She also made strategic bets on long-term technology trends. Her decision to move AMD's products to the 7-nanometer manufacturing process was risky — the technology was unproven and the transition was difficult — but it gave AMD a competitive advantage when Intel struggled with its own manufacturing process. By 2020, AMD had moved to 7nm ahead of Intel, and the company's processors were consistently rated as more power-efficient than Intel's offerings. Su's willingness to invest in manufacturing capability before it was commercially proven was a calculated risk that paid off.

## 5. THE TEST — THE BREAKTHROUGH MOMENT
The COVID-19 pandemic created an unexpected test for Su. The surge in remote work and gaming drove demand for computing power to unprecedented levels, and AMD found itself unable to meet orders. The company faced component shortages that forced it to prioritize high-margin products like server chips over consumer graphics cards. Su responded by doubling down on manufacturing partnerships — AMD relied on TSMC for chip production, and she negotiated additional capacity to meet demand. By 2021, AMD's revenue had grown to $16.4 billion, more than double what it had been when she took over.

The data center market became AMD's biggest success story. The company's EPYC processors, launched in 2017 and improved in subsequent generations, gained market share against Intel in server deployments. Google, Amazon, and Microsoft all became AMD customers for their cloud infrastructure, and the company's data center revenue grew from virtually nothing in 2016 to over $6 billion in 2021. This growth was the ultimate validation of Su's strategy: AMD had competed with Intel in the most demanding technical segment and won.

## 6. THE PHILOSOPHY — Stealable Wisdom
**On Focus:**
"Focus on what you can control and execute with excellence. Let everything else go."
**On Technical Conviction:**
"You have to believe in your technology when everyone else doubts it. That's the only way you win."
**On Execution:**
"Strategy is nothing without execution. The details matter."

## 7. THE LEGACY — Purpose Beyond Profit + Human Side
Lisa Su's legacy is the revitalization of AMD and the restoration of competition in the semiconductor industry. Before her tenure, Intel had effectively held a monopoly in x86 processors for PCs and servers, with no credible competitor to force innovation. AMD's resurgence forced Intel to accelerate its own product development and reduce prices, benefiting consumers and businesses worldwide. Su demonstrated that leadership in technology companies requires deep technical understanding, not just business acumen.

Su is married to a fellow MIT graduate, and they have no children. She is known for her calm, measured demeanor and her ability to stay focused under pressure. She has been recognized by Fortune as one of the most powerful women in business and by MIT as one of their distinguished alumni. She serves on the board of Cisco Systems and has been an advocate for increasing the representation of women and minorities in engineering. Her personal story — a Taiwanese immigrant who became CEO of one of America's most important technology companies — is a testament to the power of education and perseverance.

## 8. FINAL MOTIVATION & Personal Application Guide
**5 Actionable Steps This Week:**
1. **Focus on fewer things, do them better** — Su's turnaround began with exiting markets. List your organization's three largest initiatives and consider whether dropping one could improve the others.

3. **Make one bet before you're ready** — Su's 7nm bet was made with incomplete information. Identify one decision that has been delayed due to insufficient data and make it this week.
4. **Track operational details personally** — Su reviews yield rates and inventory personally. Identify the three metrics that matter most in your operation and review them daily for one week.
5. **Visit your customers** — Su visits customers like Microsoft and Sony personally. Identify your top three customers and schedule one visit this month to understand their needs firsthand.
