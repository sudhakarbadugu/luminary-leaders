# Phil Knight — Nike cofounder

## 1. THE HOOK
Phil Knight sold shoes from the trunk of his car in 1964, transforming that humble beginning into Nike—a company that generated $50 billion in annual revenue and became synonymous with sports culture worldwide. His 1971 slogan "Just Do It" became one of the most recognizing taglines in history. Knight's personal motto: "The cowards never started and the weak died along the way—that slogan emerged from his brutal honest assessment of entrepreneurship."

## 2. ORIGIN — THE "Before" (The Relatability Engine)
Philip David Knight was born on February 24, 1938, in their hometown of Elliot Lake, a small mining town in Ontario, Canada (his father worked for a mining company there). Within a few years, the family moved to Portland, Oregon, where Knight grew up and attended grade school and high school. Knight was an averagely competitive distance runner at Portland's Franklin High School, competing in the mile and two-mile events, though he was far from the fastest runner on his team.

Knight attended the University of Oregon, where he became a middle-distance runner on the track team under legendary coach Bill Bowerman. Bowerman was a demanding perfectionist who continuously experimented sho designs in his garage, attempting to create lighter, more responsive running shoes. Knight graduated in 1961 with a degree in journalism, then immediately enrolled in a freshly-created MBA program at Stanford University's Graduate School of Business, where he refined his business thinking alongside future leaders in a condensed program designed for early career professionals.

Knight then served in the United States Army Reserve, fulfilling his military obligation before beginning his post-graduate career. He studied accounting and business administration, skills he believed would serve him more than a purely journalistic career. Upon completing his service, Knight took a job as a certified public accountant at a major accounting firm in Portland—far from glamorous, but it gave him financial fluency he would later use to build a global shoe empire.

## 3. THE FIRE — Failures, Rock-Bottom & Turning Points
After returning from Army service, Knight traveled to Japan in 1964 with a vague business idea: to import and sell shoessource shoes in the United States. On a stopover in Kobe, Japan, he met with鬼頭 (Kito) from Onitsuka Tiger (now ASICS), the company that would become Nike's first supplier. Knight's English/Japanese business negotiation was halting at best, and Kito took a significant risk agreeing to terms with this unknown American importer.

Knight traveled back to Oregon with samples, but his first attempt to sell shoes from the trunk of his car at track meets demonstrated the brutal difficulty of building a distribution business from scratch. Runners dismissed his cheap imports as knockoffs of established brands like Adidas, and wholesale buyers at sporting goods stores were skeptical of an unknown brand lacking marketing budget. He struggled for years to gain traction, personally driving to numerous university track meets and showing shoes to coaches and athletes who barely gave him the time of day.

The partnership nearly collapsed in 1971 when Nike's first orders from Onitsuka Tiger were refused—he'd agreed to terms that created disputed invoices, and Onitsuka threatened to cut him off entirely. Knight was days from going out of business when Bowerman intervened by wiring money and helping negotiate terms. This crisis convinced Knight he needed his own brand rather than simply distributing someone else's; his company's survival depended on building something proprietary.

## 4. THE GRIND — The Invisible Years (The Method)
Knight spent the first several years as essentially a one-man distribution operation—tracking inventory on index cards, personally delivering shoes to stores in his car, and managing finances from his spare bedroom. He worked 80-hour weeks while trying to manufacture sho production quality and scale, building relationships with manufacturers in Japan and later Taiwan.

The relationship with Bowerman was foundational: Bowerman was not only a coach but the de facto head designer and innovation engine. He created Nike's iconic waffle sole pattern by pouring rubber into his wife's waffle iron, establishing Nike's reputation for innovative footwear engineering. Bowerman also provided legitimacy with elite runners, sharing the shoes with Oregon's track athletes and soon other teams who wanted the same edge. Knight brilliantly leveraged this grass-roots endorsement strategy from competitive athletes rather than paying for celebrity endorsements—his earliest marketing approach.

Knight's financial conservatism during this period proved crucial: while competitors spent heavily on marketing, Knight plowed every dollar back into product development and working capital, keeping debt manageable. His accounting background served him well here—he understood cash flow management at a time when many shoe companies were overextended. He negotiated favorable payment terms with Asian manufacturers and collected quickly from U.S. retailers, creating a financial flywheel that allowed Nike to grow without external capital.

## 5. THE TEST — THE BREAKTHROUGH MOMENT
Nike's breakthrough came with the 1972 launch of the Nike brand, coinciding with an Olympic track event where Bowerman-designed shoes gained visibility. But the true inflection point came in 1979 when Knight made the critical decision to shift Nike's marketing focus from professional athletes to everyday consumers—people who aspired to be active but didn't consider themselves competitive athletes. This insight, that sports shoes could become fashion statements, opened a mass market that competitors missed.

The 1980s brought Nike's rise to cultural dominance with Michael Jordan partnership in 1984. Knight's $2.5 million deal (which included stock options) was considered risky at the time—Jordan had just left college and hadn't yet proven himself as a professional. But Knight understood the basketball shoe market differently: Jordan wasn't just selling shoes, he was selling an aspirational image. The Air Jordan line generated $126 million in its first year, exceeding everyone's projections and creating the modern athlete endorsement model.

From there, Nike's expansion into apparel and equipment for multiple sports, plus the brand's integration with fitness culture and later streetwear, transformed a shoe company into a sports culture empire. The company navigated the criticism of the 1990s sweatshop allegations (which Knight addressed by commissioning independent audits and raising manufacturing standards) and the 2000s competition from Under Armour. Nike remained the world's most valuable sports brand, with Knight leading through these challenges until stepping down as CEO in 2006 after 31 years.

**On Persistence:**

Knight believed deeply in product innovation above all else. He frequently said that the single biggest difference between Nike and its competitors was that Nike took its design seriously. He once told shareholders that Nike would rather over-invest in product R&D than cut corners—because in the end, athletes notice when their shoes are wrong and punish the brand with their feet. Under his leadership, Nike maintained that every product rollout should contain genuine engineering innovation.

**Additional Insight:**
Knight understood branding as storytelling, not just advertising. He built Nike's identity around the heroic struggle and triumph of athletes—showing the public the darkness before the glory, not just the highlight reels. This emotional narrative approach made Nike synonymous with human achievement in a way that transcended product attributes.

## 7. THE LEGACY — Purpose Beyond Profit + Human Side
Phil Knight's legacy encompasses both business transformation and cultural influence. He didn't just build a company—he helped create the modern sports fitness movement. Nike's presence in running, training, and athletic sports created language and aspiration around personal achievement that shaped how millions approach fitness. The company democratized access to quality athletic footwear, causing running shoes to evolve from leather-and-rubber basics to engineered performance products.

Knight's contributions to global manufacturing have been complex but consequential. In the 1990s, when Nike faced intense criticism about overseas factory conditions, Knight didn't simply defend the status quo—he commissioned independent investigations, published audit results, and raised standards across his supply chain. This transparency approach was imperfect but represented a willingness to address uncomfortable truths about how global goods are manufactured.

Knight was also a major philanthropist with a particular interest in improving Portland andregon's communities. He donated hundreds of millions to Stanford's Graduate School of Business (his alma mater), the University of Oregon (his alma mater), and numerous other causes including diabetes research and fighting child exploitation. He and his wife Penny Knight have been among the largest donors to the University of Oregon, funding athletics facilities, and he funded the Knight Center for Sports Journalism at Oregon. He remains chairman of Nike's board.
