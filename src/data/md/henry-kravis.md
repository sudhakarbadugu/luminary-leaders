# Henry Kravis

**Co-Founder, Kohlberg Kravis Roberts & Co. | Private Equity Pioneer**

Henry R. Kravis is one of the most influential figures in the history of finance, having co-founded Kohlberg Kravis Roberts & Co. in 1976 and establishing private equity as a major asset class and operational approach to corporate investment. KKR, as the firm is known, pioneered the leveraged buyout—a transaction structure that allowed financial investors to acquire companies using significant borrowed capital while applying operational expertise to improve performance. The firm's landmark $31 billion acquisition of RJR Nabisco in 1988, famously chronicled in Barbarians at the Gate, became the archetype of the leveraged buyout and transformed how executives, investors, and the public understood corporate control and value creation. Kravis himself became synonymous with the excesses and achievements of the private equity era.

## Early Life and Education

Henry Kravis was born on February 5, 1948, in New York City, into a family with deep financial industry connections. His father, Ralph Kravis, was a partner at a broker-dealer firm, providing exposure to financial markets and investment concepts from an early age. This domestic environment fostered interest in business and markets, encouraging Kravis to pursue a career in finance rather than other professions that might have seemed natural given his family's resources and connections.

Kravis attended Claremont Men's College (now Claremont McKenna College) in California, graduating in 1970 with a degree in economics. The college's emphasis on practical business education provided relevant preparation for the financial career he was developing. He then attended Harvard Business School, completing an MBA in 1972, where he developed foundational knowledge in corporate finance, valuation, and management that would guide his later work. The Harvard network proved valuable—many of his classmates and colleagues would later become counterparts in the deals that defined his career.

## Entry into Private Equity

After business school, Kravis joined First National City Bank (later Citibank) in its corporate finance division. The experience provided practical training in evaluating businesses and structuring transactions, skills that would prove essential in the leveraged buyout business. He left after two years to join Bear Stearns, where he worked in the mergers and acquisitions group and gained exposure to the growing field of takeovers and restructurings that were reshaping corporate America in the 1970s.

The pivotal opportunity came when Jerome Kohlberg, whom Kravis had met at Bear Stearns, invited him to join a new investment firm. Kohlberg had been building a private investment practice focused on acquiring companies using leveraged structures, and he recognized that Kravis's analytical abilities and deal-making skills would complement his own experience. In 1976, the two partnered with George Roberts—Kohlberg's former Bear Stearns colleague—to form Kohlberg Kravis Roberts & Co., combining their capital and expertise in a firm that would define the private equity industry.

## Building KKR's Approach

KKR's strategy centered on acquiring companies using a specific structure: the firm would provide a small amount of equity capital while arranging substantial debt financing to cover the remainder of purchase prices. This leverage amplified returns when businesses performed well but also created risk if performance declined. The firm justified high debt levels by targeting companies with stable cash flows, predictable industries, and experienced management teams capable of navigating economic downturns while maintaining debt service.

Beyond financial engineering, KKR emphasized operational involvement in portfolio companies. The firm would install new management, implement efficiency improvements, and closely monitor performance through board representation and regular reporting. This hands-on approach distinguished KKR from purely financial investors who treated ownership as passive—Kravis and his partners believed that active management could generate returns that justified the premium prices paid for acquired companies.

The firm's early investments demonstrated the model's potential. Acquisition of companies like Houdaille Industries, Manufacturers Hanover, and other industrial firms generated substantial returns for investors in KKR's early funds. The track record attracted institutional investors—pension funds, insurance companies, endowments—searching for higher returns than public markets provided, providing the capital that funded larger acquisitions.

## The RJR Nabisco Acquisition

The 1988 leveraged buyout of RJR Nabisco stands as the defining transaction of the private equity era and elevated Kravis to public prominence. The deal originated when RJR Nabisco's CEO, Ross Johnson, sought to take the company private in a management buyout, but KKR's competing bid created an auction process. The resulting battle, chronicled in the bestselling book "Barbarians at the Gate," featured dramatic scenes of competing bids, intense negotiation, and the excesses of leveraged finance at its peak.

KKR ultimately acquired RJR Nabisco for approximately $31 billion—a record at the time—financing the transaction with about $20 billion of debt and $12 billion of equity. The structure left the company heavily leveraged, and the subsequent years proved challenging as rising interest rates and economic recession strained cash flows. Eventually, KKR sold its stake at substantial profit, demonstrating that leveraged buyouts could generate returns even when initial conditions deteriorated.

The RJR Nabisco deal established Kravis as the face of private equity, appearing on magazine covers and becoming a symbol of both financial innovation and perceived excess. The publicity had lasting effects—Kravis became more cautious about personal visibility and declined many interview requests in subsequent years, preferring to let the firm's investment performance rather than personal publicity define its reputation.

## Evolution of the Firm

Through subsequent decades, KKR adapted to changing market conditions while maintaining its core private equity focus. The firm expanded into other asset classes, establishing hedge funds, real estate funds, and infrastructure investments. It developed a public markets investment arm that managed funds investing in publicly traded securities. This diversification reduced dependence on the leveraged buyout cycle and provided growth opportunities as private equity markets matured.

Geographic expansion brought KKR to Europe, Asia, and other regions, establishing local teams capable of sourcing deals and managing portfolio companies across borders. The international expansion reflected both opportunity—the growth of institutional capital seeking private equity exposure—and necessity as competition for domestic deals intensified. By the 2000s, KKR had become a global firm with presence across multiple continents and asset classes.

The firm went public in 2010, listing shares on the New York Stock Exchange and converting from a private partnership to a publicly traded entity. The IPO provided liquidity for early investors and employees while allowing KKR to access public capital markets for growth funding. Kravis, who had built his personal fortune through KKR distributions, saw his net worth fluctuate with the company's stock price, which remained sensitive to private equity performance cycles.

## Philanthropy and Personal Life

Kravis has committed substantial resources to philanthropy, particularly in education and health research. The Kravis Foundation supports educational institutions including Columbia Business School, where he endowed the Kravis Center for Economic Research, and Claremont McKenna College, his undergraduate alma mater. The foundation also supports healthcare initiatives and cultural institutions in New York, where Kravis maintains his primary residence.

He has been married to Marie-Louise Kravis since 1981, who has established her own profile as an economist and commentator. The couple maintains relatively private lives despite Kravis's financial prominence, avoiding the celebrity that attached to some other private equity figures during the boom years. Their commitment to long-term giving through foundation structures reflects a broader philosophy about wealth stewardship that extends beyond immediate consumption.
