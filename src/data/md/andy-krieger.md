# Andy Krieger — The Man Who Bet More Than Existed

## 1. THE HOOK

In October 1987, after Black Monday wiped 22% off the Dow in a single day, one currency trader at Bankers Trust identified a vulnerability: the New Zealand dollar. He built a short position so large that it exceeded the entire money supply of New Zealand. By the time he was finished, he had made over $300 million for his bank — on a single trade.

How does a 32-year-old trader find the courage to bet more than a country's entire currency supply?

## 2. ORIGIN — The "Before" (The Relatability Engine)

Andy Krieger was born in the late 1950s (exact date not widely public). He graduated from Brown University and later earned an MBA from Columbia Business School. Like many traders of his generation, he entered finance at a time when global currency markets were becoming active and increasingly volatile.

He joined Bankers Trust in the early 1980s, trading currencies. Bankers Trust was not the most prestigious bank on Wall Street at the time. Its currency desk was aggressive, willing to tolerate higher-than-normal risk in exchange for higher-than-normal returns.

Krieger was quiet, private, and intensely focused — not the loud, ego-driven personality that many associate with trading floors. He was known for his preparation, his willingness to take positions that others found terrifying, and his ability to hold conviction when prices moved against him.

Before the famous New Zealand dollar trade, he had built a reputation at Bankers Trust for taking large positions. But nothing he had done prepared his colleagues for October 1987.

He was running from security. From the comfortable path of medium risk and medium reward. He wanted to see what happened when preparation met opportunity at scale.

## 3. THE FIRE — Failures, Rock-Bottom & Turning Points

October 19, 1987. Black Monday.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 22.6% in a single trading session — the largest one-day percentage decline in history. Stock markets around the world collapsed. Institutional investors were forced to liquidate positions. Central banks intervened. Panic spread to currency markets.

Most traders were focused on the equity collapse or on the most liquid currencies. Krieger looked elsewhere.

He had been studying the New Zealand dollar (NZD) for months. New Zealand, at the time, was a small economy with an even smaller currency float. The NZD was thinly traded relative to major currencies like the dollar, yen, or Deutsche mark.

Krieger believed that global funds, under pressure from equity losses, would start selling assets in smaller, more vulnerable markets. New Zealand, with its small economy and thin currency markets, was a prime target.

When selling pressure hit NZD, the currency would fall — and not just by a few percent. The lack of depth in the market meant that any significant selling would drive the price down sharply.

The turning point was the size of the position.

Krieger did not take a moderate short. He took a position so large that it exceeded the total New Zealand dollar money supply.

The trade was so big that, if New Zealand's central bank intervened to support the currency, it would deplete its foreign reserves. The country could not defend its own currency against a position of this size.

Krieger's management at Bankers Trust approved the trade. They trusted his judgment. They understood that the risk was existential for the trade, not for the bank — New Zealand could not force the position to close.

## 4. THE GRIND — The Invisible Years (The Method)

Between joining Bankers Trust in the early 1980s and October 1987, Krieger spent years studying currencies.

His method emphasized:

### Understanding Economic Fundamentals
Krieger did not trade on technical signals alone. He understood the macroeconomics of the countries whose currencies he traded: monetary supply, trade balances, central bank reserves, political conditions, and capital flows.

### Identifying Thin Markets
He knew that small markets responded more violently to pressure than large ones. A thin market amplifies any significant buying or selling pressure. If you can identify the direction of that pressure before it materializes, you can position for a move that is larger than the same magnitude of trade would produce in a deeper market.

### Accepting Volatility
He was comfortable holding positions when prices moved against him. If his fundamental thesis was correct, transient adverse price movements were noise. He would not close a trade because it was temporarily underwater.

### Scale
The most distinctive aspect of Krieger's trading was his willingness to take positions that were enormous relative to the market size. Most traders scale positions to their capital or to the market's typical volume. Krieger scaled them to his conviction.

## 5. THE TEST — The Breakthrough Moment

The short position against the New Zealand dollar in late 1987 is one of the most famous currency trades in history.

When Krieger put on the trade, the New Zealand dollar was already weakening. Global funds were selling the currency as they reduced exposure to smaller markets. The selling pressure pushed NZD down sharply.

Because New Zealand's currency market was thin, there were not enough buyers to absorb the selling. The New Zealand central bank considered intervention, but the size of the position made intervention impractical. The bank would have had to commit a substantial portion of its reserves.

The New Zealand dollar fell approximately 5-10% in the period after Krieger's short was established — a large move for a major currency, but an extreme move for a thinly traded one. Krieger covered the position at a substantial profit.

The reported profit for Bankers Trust was approximately $300 million.

For a single trade, in a single currency, in a matter of weeks.

The aftermath for Krieger was complicated. Within Bankers Trust, the trade made him a star — but also created resentment. He had earned more in a single trade than entire divisions produced in a year.

The following year, he left Bankers Trust. He joined George Soros's Quantum Fund, where he traded alongside some of the most respected currency traders in the world.

## 6. THE PHILOSOSOPHY — Stealable Wisdom

**On Conviction:**
Krieger was known for his willingness to take enormous positions when he believed he was right. The NZD short was not a gamble — it was a conclusion derived from analysis of macroeconomic fundamentals and market structure. When his thesis was correct, he sized his position to reflect his conviction.

**On Market Structure:**
He understood that markets are not equally liquid. Thin markets respond differently to large orders than deep markets. Identifying a thin market where selling pressure is about to materialize creates asymmetric risk: the downside is bounded, but the upside is amplified.

**On Accepting Adverse Price Movement:**
Traders who close positions at the first sign of an adverse tick do not survive large moves. Krieger held through temporary adverse price action because he believed his thesis was correct, not because he enjoyed the drawdown.

**On Risk Management:**
The famous trade was risky, but it was not uncontrolled. New Zealand could not force the trade to close. The country's central bank had limited reserves. Krieger understood the structural dynamics of the currency he was trading, and he knew that even if the central bank intervened, it could not hold the position open indefinitely.

**Key Principles:**
- Size positions to conviction, not just to capital
- Trade markets where structural factors amplify your thesis
- Hold through noise; close when thesis changes
- Understand the counterparty: can the other side force you out?
- Study currency flows, central bank reserves, and macro fundamentals

## 7. THE LEGACY — Purpose Beyond Profit + Human Side

After the Bankers Trust era, Krieger's public presence diminished. He continued to trade, including at George Soros's Quantum Fund. But the 1987 NZD trade remained the defining moment of his career.

In the 1990s, Krieger largely stepped away from active trading. He studied Sanskrit and Eastern philosophy. He became interested in spiritual practice and meditation.

Public details about his later life are limited. He did not write a book about trading. He did not become a television commentator or a public figure in the way that some other famous traders did.

What is known is that he treated trading as a discipline, not as a form of entertainment. When he was not trading — when the market conditions or his own research did not identify a compelling thesis — he was able to step away.

### What does he regret?

There is no public record of Krieger expressing regret about his trading or his decisions. He stepped away from the industry when he chose to.

### What does he want to be remembered for?

The 1987 NZD trade is his public legacy. It is often cited in discussions of currency markets, large positions, and the dynamics of thin markets. He has not publicly elaborated on what he wishes to be remembered for beyond the trade itself.

## 8. FINAL MOTIVATION & PERSONAL APPLICATION GUIDE

Andy Krieger's career demonstrates that the most profitable trades are found where others are not looking, sized where others are afraid, and held when others panic. His method was not magic. It was deep preparation, recognition of structural opportunity, and conviction executed at scale.

**5 Actionable Steps This Week:**

1. **Look where others aren't** — Most traders and investors focus on the most liquid, most discussed markets. The largest profits often come from overlooked markets where structural factors create asymmetric opportunities. Ask: what markets are thin, poorly understood, or ignored?

2. **Understand the counterparty's constraints** — In the NZD trade, New Zealand's central bank could not intervene effectively because its reserves were limited. When you take a position, understand: who is on the other side? What are their constraints? Can they force you to close?

3. **Size to conviction, not comfort** — Most people size positions to what feels comfortable. If your thesis has high conviction and favorable risk/reward, size the position to match your analysis, not your anxiety.

4. **Hold through noise** — Adverse price movement is not evidence of a wrong thesis. It is often liquidity, market microstructure, or temporary flow. If your macro thesis is intact, hold. Close only when the thesis changes.

5. **Step away when there is nothing to do** — Krieger stopped trading when he did not see compelling opportunities. Active trading is not always profitable trading. The willingness to do nothing is underappreciated.

**The Hard Question:**

When was the last time you sized a position to your actual conviction, rather than to what felt safe? When was the last time you spent weeks studying a single market, understanding its structural dynamics, before placing a trade?

If the answer is "never" or "a long time ago," your problem may not be your trading strategy. It may be your preparation.
