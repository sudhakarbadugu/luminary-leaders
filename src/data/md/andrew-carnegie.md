# Andrew Carnegie — Steel Empire to Philanthropy

## 1. THE HOOK

He immigrated from Scotland as a child and built the world's largest steel company. He gave away 90% of his fortune before he died. He says: "The man who dies rich dies disgraced."

How does a poor Scottish immigrant become the richest man in America—and then give it all away?

## 2. ORIGIN — THE "Before" (The Relatability Engine)

Andrew Carnegie was born November 25, 1835, in Dunfermline, Scotland. His father, William Carnegie, was a handloom weaver. His mother, Margaret Morrison, was a weaver's daughter. The family lived in a two-room cottage with no running water. Andrew started working at age five, helping his father at the loom.

The Industrial Revolution destroyed handloom weavers' livelihoods. By 1848, the family was desperate. Andrew's brother Thomas had already emigrated to America. The family decided to go.

They arrived in New York in June 1848. Then to Pittsburgh. Andrew was 12. He worked in a cotton mill for $1.20 per week. He was too small to operate the looms, so he was given the job of "spool boy"—carrying empty bobbins to the weavers. He worked 12-hour days, six days a week.

He read everything. He joined a debating society. He learned shorthand. He took night classes at the Pittsburgh Athenaeum. He worked as a telegraph operator, then as a railroad clerk.

At 17, he met Thomas A. Scott, a railroad official who became his mentor. Scott helped him get promoted. By 21, Carnegie was earning $50 per month as a division superintendent.

## 3. THE FIRE — Failures, Rock-Bottom & Turning Points

### The Panic of 1873

In 1873, Carnegie was 38 and had built a small steel empire. Then the Panic hit. Banks failed. Construction stopped. Carnegie Steel was nearly bankrupt.

Carnegie took a radical step: he bought out his partners. He borrowed heavily. He cut costs ruthlessly. He fired managers. He reduced his own salary to $2,400 per year—half what he paid himself before.

He bet everything on a new technology: the Bessemer process. It made steel 10 times faster than traditional methods. Carnegie built the largest Bessemer steel plant in the world at Edgar Thomson Works in Pittsburgh. He controlled costs better than anyone.

By 1879, Carnegie was earning $1.5 million per year. He survived the panic. His competitors did not.

### The Homestead Strike (1892)

Carnegie's steel plant in Homestead, Pennsylvania, was the largest in the world. Workers were paid well but worked brutal hours—12-hour shifts, seven days a week. The union, Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers, negotiated contracts annually.

In 1892, Carnegie Steel demanded wage cuts. The union struck. Carnegie hired the Pinkerton National Detective Agency to break the strike. 300 armed Pinkerton agents arrived on barges. Workers fought them. Seven guards and nine workers died. The state militia was called in. The strike was crushed.

Carnegie was in Scotland at the time. He wrote: "I am sorry for the workers." He returned to America and locked out the union forever. He hired only non-union workers. He built higher walls around his factories.

He was called a hypocrite. He had written "The Gospel of Wealth" (1889), arguing that the rich should give away their fortunes. Workers saw him as a brutal capitalist who crushed unions while writing articles about charity.

### The Death of His Wife (1886)

Carnegie's wife, Louise Whitfield, died in 1886 after a brief illness. They had married in 1857. They had one child, daughter Margaret (known as "Poppy"), who died at age two in 1871.

Carnegie never remarried. He was devastated. He threw himself into work. He also began giving money away in earnest—building libraries in Scotland and the United States.

## 4. THE GRIND — The Invisible Years (The Method)

Carnegie's method was vertical integration, cost control, and continuous investment.

### The Carnegie System

Carnegie owned everything: iron ore mines in Minnesota, railroads to transport ore, limestone quarries for flux, coal mines for fuel, steel mills to process it, ships to move finished products. He controlled the entire supply chain.

He drove costs down relentlessly. He built the largest blast furnaces in the world. He adopted new technologies before competitors could. He built company towns with housing, schools, and churches—all owned by Carnegie.

He paid workers well—by the standards of the time. But he paid below market. He said: "Payscale is not about fairness. It is about efficiency."

### The Library Program (1880s-1919)

Carnegie began building public libraries in 1881. He funded 2,509 libraries worldwide, including 1,679 in the United States. He spent over $50 million (over $1 billion in today's money). He required communities to provide the land and ongoing operations—a condition that sometimes created tension.

He said: "The man who dies rich dies disgraced." He gave away money while living. He believed the rich had an obligation to distribute their wealth.

### Peace and Education

Carnegie funded the Carnegie Institution for Science, the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, the Carnegie Hero Fund Commission, and the Carnegie Mellon University (founded in 1900 as Carnegie Technical Schools). He gave away over $350 million before his death.

## 5. THE TEST — THE BREAKTHROUGH MOMENT

### The Sale (1901)

J.P. Morgan approached Carnegie in 1900. Carnegie was 65. He wanted out. Morgan offered $480 million for Carnegie Steel—four times the company's annual earnings. Carnegie accepted. He signed the papers aboard his yacht in Atlanta.

The deal was announced February 23, 1901. Morgan formed United States Steel Corporation. Carnegie walked away with $225 million (approximately $7 billion today). He was the richest man in the world.

He never returned to business. He gave away $350 million in his remaining 18 years.

## 6. THE PHILOSOPHY — Stealable Wisdom

**On Wealth:**
"The man who dies rich dies disgraced."

**On Philanthropy:**
"Surplus wealth is a sacred trust which its possessor is bound to administer during his lifetime for the benefit of the community."

**On Competition:**
"Put all your eggs in one basket, then watch that basket."

**On Opportunity:**
"The growth of the steel industry has been simply enormous. And there is not a man who has made a success in it who did not see clearly where the future lay."

## 7. THE LEGACY — Purpose Beyond Profit + Human Side

Andrew Carnegie died August 11, 1919, at age 83, in Lenox, Massachusetts. He had given away over $350 million. He retained only $30 million (approximately $500 million today) for his personal expenses and family.

His legacy includes 2,509 libraries worldwide, Carnegie Mellon University, the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, and countless other institutions. The Carnegie Hall concert venue in New York City bears his name.

He was complex. He crushed unions. He wrote about the "Gospel of Wealth" while exploiting workers. He was a brutal businessman and a generous philanthropist. He was a Scottish immigrant who became the richest man in America.

He wrote: "I believe the true way to benefit mankind is not to take from the able to give to the unable, but to give the able the opportunity to develop themselves."

## 8. FINAL MOTIVATION & Personal Application Guide

Carnegie built a fortune and gave it away. His life proves wealth is a tool, not a goal.

**5 Actionable Steps This Week:**

1. **Control the chain** — Carnegie owned mines, railroads, and mills. Own what matters.

2. **Cut costs ruthlessly** — Carnegie survived the Panic of 1873 by reducing costs. Efficiency saves.

3. **Give before you die** — "The man who dies rich dies disgraced." Plan your giving now.

4. **Invest in education** — Carnegie built libraries and universities. Fund what outlasts you.

5. **Work hard in your 20s** — Carnegie worked 12-hour days, six days a week, starting at age 12. Build your foundation early.

**The Hard Question:**

Carnegie said the man who dies rich dies disgraced. How much is enough—and what will you do with the rest?
