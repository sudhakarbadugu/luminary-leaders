# Howard Schultz

**Executive Chairman, Starbucks (1987-2018) | Activist | Author**

Howard Schultz transformed Starbucks from a single Seattle coffee shop into a global phenomenon with over 30,000 stores across 80 countries, fundamentally changing how Americans understand and consume coffee. As CEO and later chairman, he built a company that demonstrated a core conviction: that business could generate profits while treating employees (whom Starbucks calls "partners") with dignity and providing customers with meaningful third-place experiences between home and work. His leadership through crises including the 2008 financial downturn and the company's expansion to thousands of new locations established him as one of the most influential business leaders of his generation, even as he drew criticism for the scale and consequences of that growth.

## Early Life and Education

Howard Schultz was born on March 19, 1953, in Brooklyn, New York, the eldest of three children in a working-class family. His father, a truck driver, suffered a leg injury on the job that left him without pay and ultimately without meaningful employment for years. The family's financial struggles during Schultz's childhood left lasting impressions about economic vulnerability and the importance of health insurance and employment benefits that would later shape his business philosophy.

He attended Northern Michigan University on a football scholarship, becoming the first person in his family to graduate from college. The experience provided not just education but exposure to ideas about business and leadership that his Brooklyn upbringing had not offered. He studied communications and business, developing an appreciation for how organizations communicated values to employees and customers—understanding that would later inform his approach to building Starbucks.

After graduating in 1975, Schultz worked briefly in the public sector before joining Xerox as a sales representative. He discovered aptitude and enjoyment in sales, developing skills in persuasion and relationship building that served his later career. He then moved to a Swedish kitchen products company, Hammarplast, where he rose to become marketing director—the first corporate management role that brought him into contact with Starbucks as a customer of Hammarplast's products.

## Discovery and Acquisition of Starbucks

In 1981, Schultz visited Starbucks in Seattle as part of his work with Hammarplast. The coffee store, founded in 1971 by Jerry Baldwin, Zev Siegl, and Gordon Bowker, operated as a retailer of whole beans and specialty coffee, not as the sit-down café that would later define the brand. Schultz was immediately captivated by the concept and the atmosphere—a place where coffee was treated as craft rather than commodity—and he joined Starbucks in 1982 as Director of Retail Operations and Marketing.

The pivotal experience came in 1983 when Schultz traveled to Italy and observed the tradition of espresso bars as community gathering places. He recognized that Starbucks could become more than a retailer if it incorporated the Italian café experience—serving espresso drinks while creating a sense of community within the stores. He convinced the owners to test an espresso bar format in the Seattle store, and the results exceeded expectations. The experience crystallized his vision for what Starbucks could become.

The opportunity to acquire Starbucks came in 1987 when the original owners decided to sell. Schultz led a group of investors who purchased the company for $3.8 million, assuming leadership as CEO. The acquisition required substantial personal financial risk—Schultz and his investors put up significant capital against uncertain prospects. But the vision was compelling: transform a regional coffee retailer into a national chain offering the Italian café experience that had proven so appealing in Seattle.

## Building the Starbucks Experience

Schultz's approach to building Starbucks centered on creating what he called the "third place"—a location that felt neither like home nor work but offered the comfort and community that both environments lacked. The design of stores, the quality of coffee, the training of partners (employees), and the general atmosphere all received careful attention. The goal was to make Starbucks a place where people would spend time, not merely conduct transactions—a strategy that increased visit frequency and revenue per customer while building the emotional connection that drove brand loyalty.

Investment in employee welfare became a signature of the Starbucks approach. In 1988, Schultz extended health insurance coverage to part-time employees working as few as twenty hours per week—a radical decision that increased labor costs but reduced turnover and attracted better employees. He believed that treating partners well would translate into better customer service, creating a virtuous cycle where employee satisfaction drove customer satisfaction and profitability. This philosophy, unusual in retail at the time, became a model that other companies eventually emulated.

The company's initial public offering in 1992 provided capital for expansion and demonstrated investor confidence in the strategy. Growth remained measured through the 1990s, with Schultz resisting pressure to expand faster than the culture could support. He closed stores in 2000 and 2001 after customer satisfaction scores declined, making the difficult decision to sacrifice short-term revenue for long-term brand preservation.

## Crisis and Reinvention

The 2008 financial crisis posed existential challenges for Starbucks. The company had expanded aggressively through the mid-2000s, with locations appearing on nearly every commercial street in America. When consumer spending collapsed, comparable store sales declined sharply, and the company faced the prospect of becoming unprofitable. The board asked Schultz to resume the CEO role he had handed off in 2000, and he accepted, prioritizing turnaround over his preference for strategic leadership.

Schultz's return coincided with an aggressive retrenchment: closing underperforming stores, reducing the workforce by over 10,000 positions, and emphasizing operational discipline over expansion. He personally closed stores for three hours in February 2008 to retrain baristas on espresso preparation—a publicity event that signaled seriousness about quality. The retrenchment worked: Starbucks stabilized and returned to growth as the economy recovered.

The turnaround established Schultz's credentials as a crisis leader and demonstrated that the company's values and commercial success could be compatible even in severe downturns. His willingness to make difficult decisions—store closures, layoffs, sacrifice of short-term revenue—while maintaining commitment to employee welfare distinguished the response from other companies that preserved shareholder returns at the expense of workers.

## Post-CEO Activities

After stepping back from the CEO role in 2018, Schultz remained active as executive chairman, focusing on innovation and social impact initiatives. He launched Starbucks "Reserve Roastery" locations, premium stores that offered immersive coffee experiences and demonstrated the brand's commitment to craft and quality. These experimental stores served as testing grounds for concepts that could eventually flow to broader Starbucks locations.

He also became increasingly involved in public policy discussions about the role of business in society. Schultz argued that companies had responsibilities extending beyond shareholder returns—that business leaders should address social problems and contribute to civic life. This perspective, sometimes characterized as "stakeholder capitalism" versus "shareholder primacy," positioned him among a group of executives arguing that the traditional model of corporate governance required revision.

## Presidential Considerations

Throughout the 2010s, speculation persisted about Schultz's potential presidential candidacy as an independent candidate. He repeatedly denied plans to run while maintaining that he was considering how he could contribute to American public life outside the two major parties. The discussions highlighted both his prominence and the sense among some voters that mainstream political options had failed to address fundamental challenges.

In 2019, Schultz announced he would not run for president, citing concerns that an independent candidacy would help reelect Donald Trump. The decision reflected strategic judgment about electoral dynamics rather than disinterest in public policy—Schultz remained engaged in advocacy around issues including veterans' support, immigration, and economic opportunity. His continued visibility in public discourse demonstrated the platform that business success had provided for social influence.

## Key Dates

- **1953**: Born in Brooklyn, New York
- **1975**: Graduated from Northern Michigan University
- **1982**: Joined Starbucks as marketing director
- **1987**: Led acquisition of Starbucks
- **1992**: Took Starbucks public
- **2000**: Stepped down as CEO (returned 2008)
- **2018**: Stepped down as CEO again; became executive chairman
