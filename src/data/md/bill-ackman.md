# Bill Ackman — The Activist Who Fights in Public

## 1. THE HOOK

He bet a billion dollars that a nutrition company was a fraud, and he lost. He bought a massive stake in a pharmaceutical company he called "the next Berkshire Hathaway," and it collapsed. But he also made over $2 billion on a bankrupt mall operator and nearly a billion on a fast-food chain recovering from a food safety crisis. Bill Ackman built Pershing Square into one of the most famous activist investment firms in the world by doing what most investors avoid: he fights his battles in public, on television, and in long slide presentations that the entire world can watch.

How does an investor survive losing billions on public bets and still come back to make billions more?

## 2. ORIGIN — The "Before" (The Relatability Engine)

William Albert Ackman was born May 11, 1966, in Chappaqua, New York, an affluent suburb of New York City. His father, Lawrence Ackman, was a real estate investor. The family was wealthy. Bill grew up with financial security but also with the expectation that he would succeed.

He attended Harvard College, graduating magna cum laude in 1988. He then went to Harvard Business School, receiving his MBA in 1992. His academic credentials were impeccable. But what he did next was unusual.

In 1992, with a partner, David Berkowitz, he founded Gotham Partners. He was 26 years old. The firm specialized in value investing and activist campaigns. Ackman made a name for himself by taking stakes in companies and pushing for changes. His first major campaign was against First Union Real Estate, a REIT he believed was undervalued and mismanaged.

He was running from a conventional path. A Harvard MBA could have gone to Goldman Sachs or any major firm. He chose to start his own fund immediately. He wanted independence and the freedom to do things his way.

## 3. THE FIRE — Failures, Rock-Bottom & Turning Points

### Gotham Partners Closure (2003)

Gotham Partners did not fail in the traditional sense. Its returns were decent. The problem was a specific investment. Ackman had invested in Gotham Golf, a golf course REIT. He proposed a merger with another REIT. The deal became complex and was ultimately rejected by shareholders.

Regulators investigated. The investigation found no wrongdoing, but the damage was done. The fund's reputation was harmed. Ackman faced redemptions. In 2003, he decided to close Gotham Partners and return capital to investors.

He had built a firm and watched it dissolve. He was in his late thirties, starting over.

### Herbalife (2012-2018)

In December 2012, Ackman gave a three-and-a-half-hour presentation at the Ira Sohn conference. His subject: Herbalife, a nutrition company he claimed was a pyramid scheme. He shorted $1 billion of Herbalife stock. He promised to donate his profits to charity.

What followed was one of the most public battles in investment history. Carl Icahn, Ackman's longtime rival, took a long position in Herbalife. They publicly insulted each other on CNBC. Herbalife's stock rose. Ackman's short lost money.

Herbalife was investigated by the FTC. It settled, paying $200 million and agreeing to restructure its business. But it was not shut down. The stock did not go to zero.

By 2018, Ackman had closed the position. The loss was estimated at over $1 billion. His reputation was damaged. Critics said he had let his ego drive his investment.

### Valeant Pharmaceuticals (2015-2017)

In 2015, Ackman invested in Valeant Pharmaceuticals. He called it a "platform company" that would compound capital like Berkshire Hathaway. He praised its CEO, Michael Pearson.

Valeant's business model—acquiring drug companies and raising prices—came under scrutiny. The company's accounting practices were questioned. Its relationship with a pharmacy, Philidor, was investigated. The stock collapsed.

Ackman's fund lost an estimated $4 billion. He publicly apologized. He sold the position. Within two years, he had lost over $5 billion on Herbalife and Valeant combined.

### The 2020 Comeback

By early 2020, Pershing Square's assets under management had declined significantly. Investors had withdrawn capital. Ackman's reputation seemed permanently damaged.

Then came COVID-19. Ackman placed a hedge—buying credit protection—that paid off dramatically when markets crashed in March 2020. The hedge made over $2 billion. He then invested the proceeds in undervalued companies, which rose as markets recovered.

In 2020, Pershing Square returned over 70%. In 2021, it acquired a stake in Netflix after it dipped; later, it sold at a loss but recouped through other positions.

In 2021-2022, Ackman's investment in Chipotle paid off. He had bought the stock after it declined due to food safety issues in 2015 and 2016. Chipotle's recovery under a new CEO generated nearly $1 billion in profit.

By 2025, Ackman had rebuilt his firm and his reputation. His net worth was estimated at $3-4 billion.

## 4. THE GRIND — The Invisible Years (The Method)

Ackman's method is activist, concentrated, and public.

### Concentrated Portfolio

Pershing Square typically holds a small number of positions—sometimes fewer than 10. This concentration means each position matters enormously. If one bet goes wrong, the impact is significant. But if one bet goes right, the returns are exceptional.

### Activist Campaigns

Ackman does not simply buy stock and wait. He pushes for changes. He demands board seats. He lobbies shareholders. He speaks to the press. He gives long presentations explaining his thesis.

This activism can create value. If he is right, the changes he pushes for can unlock value. If he is wrong, the campaign damages his reputation.

### Research-Intensive

Ackman's presentations are detailed. He and his team analyze financial statements, regulatory filings, and industry dynamics. The Herbalife presentation was 342 slides.

### Public Persona

Ackman invites publicity. He gives interviews, appears on television, and writes public letters. This public profile brings attention to his campaigns. It also brings scrutiny.

The invisible years—the years of research, the years of fighting with management teams, the years of criticism—are not glamorous. They are wars of attrition.

## 5. THE TEST — The Breakthrough Moment

### General Growth Properties (2008-2010)

In 2008, General Growth Properties, the second-largest mall operator in the United States, faced bankruptcy. Its debt load was unsustainable as credit markets froze. The stock collapsed.

Ackman bought the debt at steep discounts. He pushed for the company to restructure in bankruptcy court. He invested capital in the reorganized company.

When General Growth emerged from bankruptcy in 2010, Pershing Square's stake was worth over $2 billion. The trade was one of the most profitable in hedge fund history.

This investment showed Ackman's ability to identify value where others saw only distress.

## 6. THE PHILOSOPHY — Stealable Wisdom

**On Activism:**
Ackman believes that shareholders have the right—and the duty—to demand that management act in their interest. He does not accept passively what boards decide. He challenges them publicly.

**On Conviction:**
A concentrated portfolio requires conviction. Ackman puts enormous resources into his positions. He believes that if the work is done properly, the conviction should be strong.

**On Public Battles:**
Ackman knows that public campaigns attract attention—and criticism. He could conduct his activism privately. He chooses not to. He believes the sunlight of public scrutiny improves outcomes.

**On Resilience:**
After losing billions on Herbalife and Valeant, Ackman could have lowered his profile, changed his strategy, or retired. He persisted. His comeback was built on the same approach that had failed: concentrated, activist, public.

**Key Quotes:**
- "If you're going to be an activist, you have to be willing to be wrong publicly."
- "The most important thing in investing is not to lose money. The second most important thing is to remember the first."
- "I'm not right all the time. But when I'm right, I make a lot of money. And when I'm wrong, I lose a lot of money."

## 7. THE LEGACY — Purpose Beyond Profit + Human Side

Bill Ackman is still active. His legacy is still being written.

### Family

He married Niki Oskar in 1994. They divorced. He married Karen Herskovitz in 1998. They had four children. They divorced in 2021.

### Philanthropy

Ackman has signed the Giving Pledge. He has donated millions to education and other causes. His giving has not been as visible as his investing, but he has committed a substantial portion of his wealth.

### Controversy

In 2023, Ackman was involved in a controversy regarding Harvard, his alma mater. He publicly criticized Harvard's leadership, including the president, Claudine Gay, over issues including plagiarism allegations and responses to antisemitism on campus. The controversy was widely covered. Some praised his advocacy; others criticized his methods.

### What does he regret?

Ackman has publicly acknowledged mistakes. He said Valeant was a "huge mistake." He said Herbalife was an error—not because Herbalife was not problematic, but because he underestimated how long it could survive and how much he could lose while waiting for it to collapse.

### What does he want to be remembered for?

In interviews, he has said that he wants to be remembered as someone who created value—not just for himself, but for shareholders, employees, and communities. He has also said that he cares about his reputation and that he believes his activism improves corporate governance.

## 8. FINAL MOTIVATION & PERSONAL APPLICATION GUIDE

Bill Ackman's career demonstrates that big bets can produce big wins or big losses—and that resilience after losses is what determines long-term success. He did not change his fundamental approach after Herbalife and Valeant. He refined it and kept going.

**5 Actionable Steps This Week:**

1. **Be willing to be wrong publicly** — If you advocate for something, you will sometimes be wrong. Ackman was wrong publicly and lost billions. He kept going. Do not let the fear of public failure stop you from publicly fighting for what you believe.

2. **Concentrate where you have conviction** — Ackman does not diversify away his best ideas. If he believes in something, he makes a large investment. Identify where you have done the work. Do not dilute your effort across many areas.

3. **Do deep research before you fight** — Ackman's presentations are the result of months of work. If you are going to take a public position, do the research that allows you to defend it.

4. **Accept that losses are part of concentrated investing** — If you make large bets, some will fail. Ackman lost over $5 billion on two positions. He made even more on others. If you cannot accept large losses, do not make large bets.

5. **Persist through comebacks** — Ackman was written off. His fund was shrinking. Then 2020 happened. The key is not never to fail; the key is to fail in a way that leaves you able to continue.

**The Hard Question:**

If an investor who lost over a billion dollars on a public short and over four billion on a pharmaceutical company could still rebuild his firm, make billions more, and be recognized as one of the most influential activists in the world — what loss are you refusing to come back from?

The loss is not the end of the story. The decision to keep going is.
