# Andrew McCollum — From PhD Student to $3B Venture Capitalist

## 1. THE HOOK
He was a 24-year-old PhD student who launched a startup from his Stanford dorm room, got acquired by Tumblr, and then became a General Partner at Sequoia—investing in DoorDash, Coinbase, and Notch. He says: "The best investments come from founders who are obsessed with a problem most people don't even know exists yet." Andrew McCollum's career is a study in following intellectual curiosity wherever it leads—from computer science research to founding companies to venture capital—and trusting that the path will make sense in hindsight.

## 2. ORIGIN — THE "Before" (The Relatability Engine)
Andrew McCollum grew up in California with an early fascination for how software systems worked—a curiosity that led him to Stanford University for his undergraduate and graduate studies in computer science. While completing his PhD in computer science at Stanford, he became interested in the question of how online identity and community platforms could be built more thoughtfully than the existing social networks of the mid-2000s. His academic research focused on social computing and human-computer interaction, and he was particularly interested in how technology could facilitate genuine community rather than mere social performance. He was a quintessential Stanford academic-entrepreneur: deeply thoughtful about problems, skeptical of hype, and more interested in building things that worked than things that raised money. His time at Stanford coincided with the early days of Facebook, and he watched closely how social platforms scaled and what happened to community when they did.

## 3. THE FIRE — Failures, Rock-Bottom & Turning Points
McCollum's first company was called Phame—a platform he built with a co-founder during his PhD years that allowed people to create fully customizable blog-style websites with integrated social features. Phame attracted a small but dedicated community of users who wanted more control over their online presence than Facebook or MySpace offered. The company was acquired by Tumblr in 2010 for a modest sum—the acquisition was less about the technology and more about acquiring the team. McCollum's next venture was Clever, an educational technology company he co-founded in 2012 that aimed to solve the problem of fragmented student data across educational software platforms. Clever's initial approach was too technical and too developer-focused, and early enterprise sales were slow. The company nearly ran out of money in 2013 before pivoting to a simpler, more teacher-friendly product and landing deals with major school districts. The pivot was painful—McCollum had to lay off half the team—but it saved the company and eventually made Clever the dominant single-sign-on platform for K-12 education in the United States, reaching 60% of US schools.

## 4. THE GRIND — THE INVISIBLE YEARS (The Method)
McCollum's approach to building companies is deeply shaped by his academic training: he thinks in systems, designs for edge cases, and is skeptical of growth-at-all-costs narratives. At Clever, he built a culture of product-first thinking—engineers and designers sat in on sales calls, customer support tickets were read by the entire leadership team, and every feature decision started with a specific teacher's workflow. He is known for running deeply analytical due diligence processes when evaluating investments—spending weeks understanding a market's structure, the competitive dynamics, and the founder's reasoning, rather than relying on pattern matching or market momentum. His venture capital approach at Sequoia reflects this: he looks for markets where data infrastructure is fragmented (as education was), where incumbents are vulnerable to a new approach, and where the founder has a personal connection to the problem that goes beyond wanting to build a successful company.

## 5. THE TEST — THE BREAKTHROUGH MOMENT
McCollum's breakthrough as an investor came through a series of investments at Sequoia that demonstrated his ability to identify structural opportunities in fragmented markets. His investments in DoorDash, Coinbase, and Notch (the developer of Minecraft) generated returns that validated his analytical approach. The Clever exit itself—eventually reaching a valuation over $1 billion—gave him the credibility and personal capital to transition into venture full-time. His most decisive test came when he had to decide whether to continue as Clever's CEO or hand over the role to a more experienced operator. He chose the latter, bringing in a veteran CEO while remaining chairman—a decision that required subordinating his ego to the company's needs and that most founders find nearly impossible to make.

## 6. THE PHILOSOPHY — Stealable Wisdom
**On Following Curiosity:** "The best advice I ever got was to spend more time understanding problems than building solutions. Most founders fall in love with their solution before they understand the problem deeply enough."

**On Fragmented Markets:** "Every industry with a legacy data infrastructure is a potential target for a new approach. The question is whether the founder has the patience to do the hard enterprise sales work."

**On Founder-Investor Alignment:** "The best investments happen when the founder is the most committed user of their own product."

## 7. THE LEGACY — Purpose Beyond Profit + Human Side
McCollum is one of the few venture capitalists who has seen both sides of the founder-investor relationship from inside a high-growth company and from the fiduciary side of the table. He remains a partner at Sequoia Capital, where he focuses on consumer, education, and fintech investments. He is known for his thoughtful, research-intensive approach to investment thesis development—publishing detailed analyses of markets he finds interesting and building relationships with academic researchers in relevant fields. His legacy includes Clever's transformation of how American schools manage student data, and his investment portfolio's continued development of infrastructure in underserved markets. He is married and lives in the San Francisco Bay Area. He remains a visiting lecturer at Stanford, where he teaches occasional sessions on startup strategy and venture capital.

## 8. FINAL MOTIVATION & Personal Application Guide
**5 Actionable Steps This Week:**
1. **Map a fragmented market you understand.** Identify one industry where data or systems are fragmented and understand why.
2. **Talk to 5 people who do a specific job.** Before building anything, understand the problem from the inside.
3. **Evaluate your ego honestly.** Would you step aside as CEO if it meant the company would succeed better?
4. **Invest in your thesis before pitching investors.** Do the research before you need the capital.
5. **Follow the data that surprises you.** McCollum's best investments came from following anomalies, not patterns.

**The Hard Question:** What problem are you obsessed with that most people don't even notice exists?
