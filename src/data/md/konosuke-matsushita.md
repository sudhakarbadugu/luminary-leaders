# Konosuke Matsushita

## Origins and Early Struggles

Konosuke Matsushita was born on November 27, 1894, in Wakayama, Japan, the third of seven children in a family that struggled financially during Japan's rapid industrialization. His father, Matsushita Konosuke, owned a small bicycle shop that went bankrupt when Konosuke was young, forcing the family into significant hardship. At age 9, he began working to help support his family, taking jobs as a messenger boy and later as an apprentice in a local hardware store. These early experiences with poverty and economic instability shaped his philosophy of customer-focused business development and persistence in the face of adversity.

## Entry into Electrical Business

At age 16, Matsushita joined the Osaka Electric Light Company as an assistant in the technical department, beginning a seven-year journey that would teach him the electrical industry from the ground up. During his employment, he developed several product improvements in his spare time, eventually receiving patents for his innovations. In 1918, he left his secure job to start his own company with his wife and a small group of collaborators, renting a modest facility to begin producing light sockets and other electrical components. His timing coincided with Japan's rapid electrification of homes and businesses.

## Founding Matsushita Electric

Matsushita founded Matsushita Electric Housewares Manufacturing in 1918 with capital of just 100 yen and a small team of workers. His early products included light sockets and telephone connectors, which he sold to electrical companies throughout Japan. The company grew slowly but consistently through the 1920s, surviving economic downturns by maintaining strict financial discipline and focusing on consumer needs. By the late 1920s, Matsushita had expanded into battery-powered devices and radio components, positioning the company for the consumer electronics boom that would follow World War II.

## War Years and Post-War Recovery

During World War II, Matsushita's company was converted to produce military equipment, including radio components and aircraft parts. The war devastated Japan's economy, and Matsushita faced the challenge of rebuilding his company from ruins. He made the bold decision to acquire competitor companies and hire workers from competitors who had gone bankrupt, positioning Matsushita Electric for rapid post-war expansion. By the late 1940s, the company had recovered and was producing a wider range of consumer products for the rebuilding Japanese market.

## Rise to National Prominence

In 1950, Matsushita began expanding internationally, establishing partnerships with Philips and eventually opening sales offices throughout Asia and later Europe and America. The company adopted the National brand for its consumer products and Panasonic for electronics, creating distinct identities for different market segments. Matsushita's leadership guided the company through Japan's remarkable economic growth in the 1950s and 1960s, making Matsushita Electric one of the largest employers and most valuable companies in Japan.

## Management Philosophy and Practices

Matsushita developed a distinctive management philosophy that emphasized customer satisfaction, employee empowerment, and continuous improvement. He famously declared that manufacturers have a responsibility to make products that are "as cheap as water" and available to everyone, driving the company's focus on manufacturing efficiency and cost reduction. He practiced a style of management that delegated authority to division managers while maintaining overall strategic direction. His belief in the potential of Japanese workers and their capacity for innovation distinguished him from contemporaries who doubted domestic capabilities.

## Transition and Succession Planning

In 1973, Matsushita stepped down as president at age 79, transitioning to the role of chairman while his adopted son Masaharu Matsushita took over leadership. He spent his later years focusing on cultural and educational activities, establishing foundations to promote business ethics and youth development. His succession planning included developing a management structure that could survive his eventual death while maintaining the company's values and culture. The company continued to thrive under subsequent generations of Matsushita family leadership and professional managers.

## Legacy and Historical Significance

Konosuke Matsushita died on April 18, 1989, at age 94, having built a company that would become one of the largest consumer electronics manufacturers in the world. His contributions to Japanese industrial development demonstrated how entrepreneurship and management innovation could transform a small workshop into a global enterprise. His management writings, including "Matsushita Leadership" and various essays, continue to influence business education in Japan and internationally. The company he founded, eventually renamed Panasonic Corporation, remains a major global technology company with revenues exceeding $60 billion.
