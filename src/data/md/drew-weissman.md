# Drew Weissman

**Nobel Laureate in Physiology or Medicine, mRNA Vaccine Pioneer**

---

## Section 1: Early Life and Academic Path

Drew Weissman was born in 1959 in Lexington, Massachusetts, the son of a steel company executive and a schoolteacher. He grew up in a family that valued education and public service, and his path toward medicine was relatively direct—he studied at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, where he completed both his undergraduate degree and his MD through the school's combined medical scientist training program. The program was designed for students who wanted to conduct research as well as practice medicine, and Weissman's career would embody that dual commitment throughout its duration.

He completed his residency at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, one of Harvard Medical School's teaching hospitals, and then went to the US National Institutes of Health for a fellowship. It was at the NIH, in the laboratory of Dr. Anthony Fauci, that his career would take its most consequential turn. He arrived as a trained immunologist with interests in infectious disease, equipped with the tools to ask questions about how the immune system recognizes and responds to pathogens. What he found would change not just his career but the course of medicine itself.

## Section 2: The Meeting That Changed Medicine

At the NIH in the late 1990s, Weissman encountered Katalin Karikó, a Hungarian-born researcher who had dedicated her career to an idea that seemed almost impossible—that messenger RNA could be used to instruct the human body to produce its own therapeutic proteins. The concept had seemed promising in theory but had run into a fundamental obstacle: the human immune system recognized synthetic mRNA as foreign and attacked it, triggering inflammatory responses that made the technology impractical for therapy. Karikó had been working on the problem for years, publishing papers that received little attention and struggling to maintain her career in an environment that viewed her ideas as fringe.

Weissman, newly arrived and curious about unexplored areas of immunology, met Karikó at a photocopier in the NIH corridor—an encounter that colleagues would later describe as one of the most consequential in modern medical history. They began collaborating almost immediately, drawn together by complementary expertise and shared curiosity. Karikó understood the mRNA technology; Weissman understood the immune system. Together, they could ask questions neither could answer alone.

## Section 3: The 2005 Breakthrough

Weissman and Karikó began collaborating on the mRNA problem, conducting experiments in the NIH lab that would eventually prove transformative. Their key insight came from studying how the body distinguishes its own RNA from foreign RNA. They discovered that substituting pseudouridine—a modified nucleoside found naturally in human RNA—into synthetic mRNA dramatically reduced the immune system's inflammatory response. The modification essentially tricked the body into accepting the engineered mRNA as endogenous, allowing it to deliver its instructions without triggering an attack.

Their research was published in the journal Immunity in 2005 and initially received little attention. The scientific community was focused on other approaches to vaccine development, and mRNA remained a curiosity. It would take more than a decade, and the urgency of a global pandemic, for the world to understand what they had done. The breakthrough sat in the literature, underappreciated and unused, while the world went about the business of medicine as usual. Sometimes the most important discoveries arrive before their moment, waiting for circumstances to reveal their significance.

## Section 4: The Long Road to Recognition

Weissman rose to become director of the University of Pennsylvania's Institute for RNA Innovations, where his lab continued working on mRNA-based vaccines and therapeutics. The work was scientifically satisfying but practically invisible—no pharmaceutical company was interested in mRNA technology, grant funding was scarce, and the research appeared to be a scientific backwater with no commercial application. Karikó eventually left Penn, moving to a German company called BioNTech that few had heard of outside scientific circles.

Weissman stayed at Penn, continuing to refine the technology and train the next generation of RNA researchers. He was content, he said later, to work in the margins of mainstream science if the work was interesting and potentially useful. His lab produced a steady stream of papers on mRNA technology, each building on the previous work, each adding to the understanding of how the modifications worked and how they could be improved. The patience was rewarded in ways neither he nor Karikó could have predicted, but both had developed over years of working on ideas that the mainstream considered speculative.

## Section 5: COVID-19 and the Vaccine Revolution

When COVID-19 emerged in early 2020, the race to develop vaccines became the defining scientific challenge of the era. Both Moderna and Pfizer-BioNTech turned to the modified mRNA approach that Weissman and Karikó had pioneered, designing vaccines that used synthetic mRNA to instruct cells to produce the coronavirus's spike protein, training the immune system to recognize it. The vaccines achieved unprecedented efficacy—over 90%—and were developed in less than a year, shattering all previous records for vaccine development. The speed was possible because the modified mRNA platform was flexible: once the viral genome was published, scientists could design a new vaccine candidate in days.

The technology's success validated decades of work by Weissman and Karikó, and their method became the foundation for the most effective vaccines in history. The vaccines saved millions of lives and enabled the reopening of economies that had been shut down by the pandemic. The speed of development—less than a year from the identification of the virus to emergency authorization—represented a triumph of scientific ingenuity and public investment. The fact that it rested on discoveries made fifteen years earlier illustrated the unpredictable nature of scientific progress: you cannot know which basic research will prove decisive until it does.

## Section 6: The Nobel Prize and Its Meaning

In October 2023, Weissman and Karikó received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for their discoveries concerning nucleoside base modifications that enabled mRNA vaccines. The award was described by the Nobel Committee as transformative—fundamentally changing humanity's ability to fight disease. The recognition came after years of other prizes and awards that had begun to accumulate as the significance of their work became undeniable. The Lasker Award, the Breakthrough Prize, and others had preceded the Nobel, each acknowledging a different dimension of their contribution.

Weissman's response was characteristically modest: he said he was surprised but pleased, and that the prize belonged to the many researchers who had worked on mRNA over the decades. He acknowledged that the recognition would help attract more funding to RNA research but emphasized that the work was not finished. His lab at Penn continued working on vaccines for cancer, flu, and other pathogens, and he remained convinced that mRNA technology had applications far beyond what had so far been achieved. The Nobel was not an ending but a recognition that created new responsibilities.

## Section 7: Ongoing Research and Public Engagement

Weissman continues his research at Penn, where his lab works on vaccines for cancer, HIV, and other pathogens. The mRNA platform has proven remarkably flexible, adaptable to different antigens and different diseases. Cancer vaccines, which train the immune system to attack tumor cells, represent one of the most promising applications, and several are already in clinical trials. The ability to customize mRNA sequences means that the platform can be adapted to new targets faster than traditional vaccine approaches, making it ideal for responding to emerging pathogens.

He remains somewhat bewildered by fame. He prefers the bench to the spotlight and has never been comfortable with the celebrity that his discovery has brought. He keeps a photograph of himself with Karikó in his office—a reminder, he says, that scientific discovery is often a partnership before it is a prize. He has testified before Congress and become a public advocate for vaccine science, arguing that the COVID pandemic demonstrated both the power of scientific investment and the dangers of underestimating infectious disease threats. His core message—that breakthroughs can take decades but that society must be prepared to move fast when they arrive—has become one of the most important lessons of the 21st century.

## Section 8: Legacy and Future Implications

The impact of Weissman's discovery extends far beyond COVID. The mRNA platform represents a new approach to medicine—one in which the body is instructed to produce the proteins it needs rather than having them delivered from outside. This approach could transform treatment for cancer, genetic diseases, and infectious diseases in ways that are only beginning to be explored. The technology has matured rapidly since 2020, with billions of dollars invested in manufacturing capacity and research programs that will keep the field advancing for decades.

Weissman's career embodies the value of basic research conducted for its own sake, without immediate commercial application in view. The discovery that made COVID vaccines possible was made in 2005, seventeen years before the pandemic demonstrated its significance. The lesson—that society must invest in basic science even when it cannot predict where the discoveries will lead—has been repeated many times but rarely with such dramatic illustration. He is married with three children and maintains that the most important thing about any scientific breakthrough is what it allows you to do for patients, not the recognition it brings to the researchers.
